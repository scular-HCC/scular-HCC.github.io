Phasors &
Discrete-Time Sinusoids
The stationary phasor compresses an entire sinusoid into one complex number. Then we jump from $t$ to $n$ — and time disappears.
Reading · Textbook §1.4 – §1.5
Lecture 04Objectives
By the end of this lecture you will

Read sinusoids as phasors,
and frequencies as angles per sample.

  1. Write $A\cos(\omega t + \phi)$ as $\Re\{A e^{j\phi}\, e^{j\omega t}\}$ — the stationary phasor is $A e^{j\phi}$.
  2. Add sinusoids of the same frequency by adding their phasors.
  3. Recognise that DT frequencies $\Omega$ and $\Omega + 2\pi$, or $\Omega$ and $-\Omega$, are equivalent — the effective range is $[0, \pi]$.
  4. Determine when a DT sinusoid is periodic: $\Omega = (k/N)\,2\pi$.

ConceptStationary Phasor
Stationary phasor

One complex number
captures amplitude and phase.

$$x(t) \,=\, A\cos(\omega t + \phi) \,=\, \Re\!\left\{ A e^{j\phi} \cdot e^{j\omega t} \right\}$$
$$\text{Stationary phasor: } \;\; A e^{j\phi}$$

Symbols: $A$ amplitude, $\omega$ angular frequency (rad/s), $\phi$ phase (rad), $j=\sqrt{-1}$, $\Re\{\cdot\}$ real part.

Why it works. Euler's formula $e^{j\theta}=\cos\theta+j\sin\theta$ gives $\cos\theta=\Re\{e^{j\theta}\}$. With $\theta=\omega t+\phi$, and the exponent rule $e^{a+b}=e^{a}e^{b}$:

$$A\cos(\omega t+\phi)=\Re\{A e^{j(\omega t+\phi)}\}=\Re\{A e^{j\phi}e^{j\omega t}\}$$

$A e^{j\phi}$ is the rotating vector $z(t)=A e^{j(\omega t+\phi)}$ frozen at $t=0$.

ConceptSum of Same-Frequency Sinusoids
Adding sinusoids

Same frequency in,
same frequency out.

$$\begin{aligned}&A_1\cos(\omega t + \phi_1) + A_2\cos(\omega t + \phi_2)\\ &=\Re\{A_1e^{j\phi_1}e^{j\omega t}\}+\Re\{A_2e^{j\phi_2}e^{j\omega t}\}\\ &=\Re\{(A_1e^{j\phi_1}+A_2e^{j\phi_2})\,e^{j\omega t}\}\\ &=\Re\{A e^{j\phi}\,e^{j\omega t}\}=A\cos(\omega t+\phi)\end{aligned}$$ $$\text{where }\; A e^{j\phi} \,=\, A_1 e^{j\phi_1} + A_2 e^{j\phi_2}$$

Line 2: each cosine written as $\Re\{\cdot\}$ (Euler).

Line 3: $\Re\{u\}+\Re\{v\}=\Re\{u+v\}$, then factor the common $e^{j\omega t}$. The frequency $\omega$ never enters the algebra.

Back to polar. If the phasor sum is $P=a+jb$, then $A=|P|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$ and $\phi=\angle P=\operatorname{atan2}(b,a)$ (the quadrant of $a,b$ decides $\phi$; a bare $\arctan(b/a)$ can be off by $\pi$).

Worked ExampleCombine via Phasors (1 of 2)
Worked example

Combine into a single cosine.

$$x(t)=2.7\cos(15\pi t + 0.6) + 4.1\sin(15\pi t - 1.8)$$

Both terms have $\omega = 15\pi$ rad/s, so phasor addition applies once the sine is a cosine.

Step 1 · Convert sine to cosine
$$\sin\theta = \cos(\theta - \tfrac{\pi}{2})$$ $$4.1\sin(15\pi t - 1.8) = 4.1\cos(15\pi t - 1.8 - \tfrac{\pi}{2})$$ $$-1.8-\tfrac{\pi}{2}=-1.8-1.5708=-3.3708$$
Step 2 · Stationary phasors
$$P_1 = 2.7\,e^{j0.6},\qquad P_2 = 4.1\,e^{-j3.3708}$$

Rectangular form from $a e^{j\theta}=a\cos\theta+j\,a\sin\theta$:

$$\begin{aligned}P_1&=2.7(0.8253)+j\,2.7(0.5646)\\&=2.2284+1.5245j\\ P_2&=4.1(-0.9738)+j\,4.1(0.2272)\\&=-3.9928+0.9315j\end{aligned}$$
Where did the sine go?

It became a phase-shifted cosine, so both signals are now $\Re\{P\,e^{j15\pi t}\}$ and their phasors can be added.

Worked ExampleCombine via Phasors (2 of 2)
Worked example, continued

Sum, then polar form.

Step 3 · Sum
$$\begin{aligned}P&=P_1+P_2\\&=(2.2284-3.9928)+j(1.5245+0.9315)\\&=-1.7644+2.4561\,j\end{aligned}$$
Step 4 · Polar form
$$\begin{aligned}A&=\sqrt{(-1.7644)^2+2.4561^2}\\&=\sqrt{3.1131+6.0324}=3.0241\end{aligned}$$ $$\phi=\pi-\arctan\tfrac{2.4561}{1.7644}=\pi-0.9478=2.1937\ \text{rad}$$

Real part negative, imaginary part positive: second quadrant, hence $\pi-\arctan$.

Result
$$x(t) \,=\, 3.0241\cos(15\pi t + 2.1937)$$

Check at $t=0$: $2.7\cos0.6+4.1\sin(-1.8)=2.2284-3.9928=-1.7644$ and $3.0241\cos2.1937=-1.7644$. ✓ Note $A\neq 2.7+4.1$.

Notebook check: the dashed red single cosine lies exactly on the blue sum of two terms; the peak height is $A\approx3.02$, not $2.7+4.1$.

ConventionFrequency Equivalence
Which frequencies are the same?

Mod $2\pi$. And $\Omega \leftrightarrow -\Omega$
for real-valued signals.

Add a full turn (complex or real)
$$e^{j(\Omega + 2\pi)n} = e^{j\Omega n}\, e^{j 2\pi n} = e^{j\Omega n}$$

because $e^{j2\pi n}=\cos 2\pi n+j\sin 2\pi n=1$ for every integer $n$.

Sign flip (real only)
$$\cos(\Omega n + \phi) = \cos(-\Omega n - \phi)$$

because $\cos(-\theta)=\cos\theta$: frequency and phase change sign.

Effective range for real DT sinusoids
$$\Omega \in [0,\, \pi] \qquad 0 = \text{lowest (DC)},\;\; \pi = \text{highest (alternating)}$$

Recipe: subtract multiples of $2\pi$ until $\Omega\in[0,2\pi)$. If $\Omega>\pi$, replace it by $2\pi-\Omega$ (that is $-\Omega$ plus a full turn) and negate $\phi$.

Worked ExampleReduce to the Standard Range
Worked example

Find the smallest equivalent $\Omega$.

Complex case
$$z[n] = e^{j(37\pi/13\, n \,+\, \pi/8)}$$

$37\pi/13\approx2.85\pi>2\pi$. Subtract one full turn, $2\pi = 26\pi/13$ (allowed because $e^{-j2\pi n}=1$):

$$\tfrac{37\pi}{13}-\tfrac{26\pi}{13}=\tfrac{11\pi}{13}$$ $$z[n] = e^{j(11\pi/13\, n \,+\, \pi/8)}$$

A complex exponential cannot use the sign flip, so $[0,2\pi)$ is the end of the line; the phase $\pi/8$ is untouched.

Real case
$$x[n] = \cos\!\left(\tfrac{42\pi}{13}\, n - \tfrac{5\pi}{6}\right)$$

(a) Subtract $2\pi=\tfrac{26\pi}{13}$: $\tfrac{42\pi}{13}-\tfrac{26\pi}{13}=\tfrac{16\pi}{13}\approx1.23\pi>\pi$, still too big.

(b) $\cos\theta=\cos(-\theta)$: $x[n]=\cos\!\left(-\tfrac{16\pi}{13}n+\tfrac{5\pi}{6}\right)$.

(c) Add $2\pi$ to the frequency: $-\tfrac{16\pi}{13}+\tfrac{26\pi}{13}=\tfrac{10\pi}{13}\approx0.77\pi\in[0,\pi]$.

$$x[n]=\cos\!\left(\tfrac{10\pi}{13}\, n + \tfrac{5\pi}{6}\right)$$

Check $n=1$: both forms give $0.3167$.

SolutionEndpoints of $[0, \pi]$
Lowest frequency: DC

$\Omega=0$:

$$x[n] = A\cos(0\cdot n+\phi)=A\cos\phi$$

A constant sequence. Every sample takes the same value $A\cos\phi$; the signal does not oscillate at all.

Highest frequency: alternating

$\Omega=\pi$. Angle-addition: $\cos(a+b)=\cos a\cos b-\sin a\sin b$.

$$\begin{aligned}x[n]&=A\cos(\pi n)\cos\phi-A\sin(\pi n)\sin\phi\\&=A(-1)^{n}\cos\phi-0\end{aligned}$$

since $\cos\pi n=(-1)^n$ and $\sin\pi n=0$ for integer $n$. The sequence is $+,-,+,-,\ldots$ times $A\cos\phi$, the fastest oscillation possible.

ConceptPeriodicity of $x[n]$
Discrete-time periodicity

Periodic only if $\Omega/2\pi$
is rational.

$$\Omega \,=\, \tfrac{k}{N}\,2\pi$$

$x[n]$ is periodic with period $N$ if $x[n+N]=x[n]$ for all $n$. Now $x[n+N]=\cos(\Omega n+\Omega N+\phi)$, which equals $x[n]$ for every $n$ exactly when $\Omega N=2\pi k$ for an integer $k$.

The smallest positive integer $N$ that works is the fundamental period. It is not simply $2\pi/\Omega$, which is rarely an integer.

If $\Omega/2\pi$ is irrational, no integer $N$ works: $x[n]$ is aperiodic, though it still looks oscillatory.

ConceptENES-222 · L04
DerivationWhy N Is the Denominator
Derivation

$N$ is the denominator.

Write $\Omega/2\pi=p/q$ with integers $p,q$ that have no common factor.

Step 1 · What periodic means
$$x[n+N]=x[n]\ \ \forall n\iff\Omega N=2\pi m,\ \ m\in\mathbb{Z}$$
Step 2 · Substitute
$$\tfrac{2\pi p}{q}\,N=2\pi m\iff\tfrac{pN}{q}=m$$

So $pN/q$ must be an integer.

Step 3 · No common factor

$q$ divides $pN$. Since $q$ shares no factor with $p$, $q$ must divide $N$ itself, so $N=q,\,2q,\,3q,\ldots$

Step 4 · Smallest choice
$$N=q\qquad(m=p\ \text{then works})$$
Example

$\Omega=0.3\pi$:

$$\tfrac{\Omega}{2\pi}=0.15=\tfrac{3}{20}\ \Rightarrow\ N=20$$

Why not $N=10$? Then $pN/q=30/20=1.5$, not an integer. If $\Omega/2\pi$ is irrational no fraction $p/q$ exists: no $N$ works.

ApplicationBalanced Three-Phase Power
Application · ≈ 3 min

Three phases cancel.

Power lines carry three voltages with the same $\omega$, shifted by a third of a turn:

$$v_k(t)=\cos\!\left(\omega t-\tfrac{2\pi k}{3}\right),\ \ k=0,1,2$$
Step 1 · Phasors
$$\begin{aligned}P_0&=1\\P_1&=e^{-j2\pi/3}=-\tfrac12-j\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\\P_2&=e^{-j4\pi/3}=-\tfrac12+j\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\end{aligned}$$
Notebook figure. Left: the three phasors head to tail close into a triangle. Right: three cosines ($\omega=2\pi\cdot60$ rad/s) and their sum, identically zero.
Step 2 · Sum
$$\begin{aligned}\Re P&=1-\tfrac12-\tfrac12=0,\qquad\Im P=-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}+\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}=0\end{aligned}$$

$P=0$, so $v_0+v_1+v_2=\Re\{0\cdot e^{j\omega t}\}=0$ for every $t$. Equal loads: the three currents cancel and the neutral wire carries nothing.

PitfallsCommon Mistakes
Common mistakes

Six ways this lecture goes wrong.

01 · AMPLITUDES
$A\neq A_1+A_2$
Add phasors, then take $|P|$: $2.7+4.1=6.8$ but $A=3.02$.
02 · SINE
$\sin\theta=\cos(\theta-\tfrac{\pi}{2})$
Subtract $\pi/2$, not add, before forming the phasor.
03 · QUADRANT
$\arctan(b/a)$ loses the quadrant.
$P=-1.76+2.46j$: $\arctan=-0.95$ but $\phi=2.19$.
04 · PHASE FLIP
$\Omega\to2\pi-\Omega$ means $\phi\to-\phi$.
Forgetting the sign change gives different samples.
05 · PERIOD
$N\neq2\pi/\Omega$ in general.
Reduce $\Omega/2\pi=k/N$: for $3\pi/7$, $2\pi/\Omega=2.33$ but $N=14$.
06 · SYMBOLS
$\omega$ is rad/s; $\Omega$ is rad/sample.
$x[n]$ has no seconds in it; do not call $\Omega$ a frequency in Hz.

Lecture 04Summary
Take with you

Four facts.

01 · PHASOR
$A\cos(\omega t + \phi) \leftrightarrow A e^{j\phi}$
Phasors live on $\mathbb{C}$; add them like vectors.
02 · DT SINUSOID
$x[n] = A\cos(\Omega n + \phi)$
$\Omega$ is radians per sample. No time.
03 · EQUIVALENCE
$\Omega \equiv \Omega + 2\pi$; $\Omega \equiv -\Omega$ (real).
Effective range: $[0, \pi]$.
04 · PERIODICITY
$\Omega = (k/N)\, 2\pi$
Smallest $N$ is the fundamental period.
