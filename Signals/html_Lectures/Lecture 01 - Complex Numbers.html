Complex Numbers
A complex number is just a point on a plane. Everything in this course — sinusoids, phasors, sampling, transforms — sits on top of that one idea.
Lecture 01Objectives
By the end of this lecture you will

Locate, describe, and convert

  2. Read and write $z$ in either Cartesian form $(x,y)$ or polar form $(r,\theta)$.

ConceptThe Complex Plane
Concept

The complex plane.

A complex number $z$ is a single point — equivalently a vector from the origin — on a two-dimensional plane.

We call that plane the complex plane and write it $\mathbb{C}$.

Here $x$ and $y$ are the horizontal and vertical coordinates of the point, $r$ is its distance from the origin, and $\theta$ is the angle of the arrow, measured from the positive horizontal axis.

Two equivalent addresses for $z$

Cartesian: $z = (x, y)$  ·  Polar: $z = (r, \theta)$.

FIG. 1 · A complex number $z$ on the plane $\mathbb{C}$.
ConceptENES-222 · L01
ConceptCartesian Coordinates
Cartesian form

$z$ has a real part
and an imaginary part.

Real part$\ x \;=\; \mathrm{Re}\{z\}$
Imaginary part$\ y \;=\; \mathrm{Im}\{z\}$

We write $z = x + jy$, where $j=\sqrt{-1}$ is the imaginary unit (engineers use $j$ because $i$ means current). The two coordinate axes of $\mathbb{C}$ are the real axis (horizontal) and the imaginary axis (vertical).

Reading the notation

"$\mathrm{Re}\{z\}=-5$" means the point sits five units to the left of the origin; the imaginary axis tells you how far up or down.

FIG. 2 · Cartesian coordinates project onto the real and imaginary axes.
ConceptENES-222 · L01
ConceptPolar Coordinates
Polar form

$z$ has a length
and a direction.

Modulus$\ r \;=\; |z|$
Angle$\ \theta \;=\; \angle z$

The modulus $r$ is the distance from the origin to $z$ — never negative. The angle $\theta$ is measured counter-clockwise from the positive real axis, in radians.

Why two coordinate systems?

Addition is easy in Cartesian. Multiplication, rotation, and the connection to sinusoids will be easy in polar. We keep both.

FIG. 3 · Polar coordinates: length $r$ and angle $\theta$.
ConceptENES-222 · L01
ConversionPolar → Cartesian
Polar to Cartesian

Drop the right triangle.

$$\;x \,=\, r\cos\theta\;$$
$$\;y \,=\, r\sin\theta\;$$

Read the polar form as the hypotenuse of a right triangle:

  • The adjacent side, projected onto the real axis, has length $r\cos\theta$.
  • The opposite side, rising along the imaginary axis, has length $r\sin\theta$.

No sign correction needed — $\cos$ and $\sin$ already carry it.

ConversionENES-222 · L01
ConversionCartesian → Polar
Cartesian to polar

Pythagoras, then arctangent —
with one quadrant correction.

$$\;r \,=\, \sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}\;$$
$$\;\theta \,=\, \arctan\!\left(\tfrac{y}{x}\right)\;$$
add $\pi$ if and only if $x < 0$

$r$ is the hypotenuse of the right triangle with legs $|x|$ and $|y|$ (Pythagoras), and $\tan\theta=y/x$ because $y/x=(r\sin\theta)/(r\cos\theta)$.

The bare $\arctan$ function returns angles in $(-\tfrac{\pi}{2},\tfrac{\pi}{2})$ — the right half-plane.

When $x<0$ the point is in the left half-plane, so add $\pi$ to land in the correct quadrant.

If $x=0$ the ratio $y/x$ is undefined: $\theta=+\pi/2$ for $y>0$ and $-\pi/2$ for $y<0$. In code, use atan2(y, x) — it handles the correction automatically.

ConversionENES-222 · L01
ConventionAngles & Radians
Angle conventions

Always radians.
Equivalent mod $2\pi$.

$$\;2\pi \text{ rad} \;=\; 360^{\circ}\;$$

Two angles that differ by an integer multiple of $2\pi$ point in the same direction. They name the same complex number.

"mod $2\pi$" means: add or subtract whole turns of $2\pi$ until the angle lands in the chosen range, e.g. $\tfrac{7\pi}{3}\bmod 2\pi=\tfrac{\pi}{3}$.

Standard ranges

By convention we restrict $\theta$ to either $[\,0,\, 2\pi\,)$ or $(-\pi,\,\pi\,]$. Pick one and stay consistent within a problem.

Worked ExamplePlot z₁ and z₂
Problem

Plot $z_1$ and $z_2$
on the complex plane.

$z_1$
$\mathrm{Re}\{z_1\} = -5,\quad \mathrm{Im}\{z_1\} = 2$
$z_2$
$|z_2| = 4,\quad \angle z_2 = \tfrac{\pi}{6}$
Step 1 · Convert the first number to polar form
$$|z_1| = \sqrt{(-5)^{2} + 2^{2}} = \sqrt{25+4} = \sqrt{29} \approx 5.385$$ $$\arctan\!\left(\tfrac{2}{-5}\right) = \arctan(-0.4) = -0.3805 \text{ rad}$$ $$\theta_1 = -0.3805 + \pi = 2.7611 \text{ rad} = 2.7611\cdot\tfrac{180^{\circ}}{\pi} = 158.2^{\circ}$$

$\pi$ is added because $x<0$ (second quadrant).

Step 2 · Convert the second number to Cartesian form
$$\mathrm{Re}\{z_2\} = 4\cos\tfrac{\pi}{6} = 4\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} = 2\sqrt{3} \approx 3.464$$ $$\mathrm{Im}\{z_2\} = 4\sin\tfrac{\pi}{6} = 4\cdot\tfrac{1}{2} = 2$$
FIG. · Notebook plot of $z_1$ and $z_2$. $z_1$ sits left of the imaginary axis, so its angle is more than $90^{\circ}$ ($158.2^{\circ}$); $z_2$ sits $30^{\circ}$ above the real axis at length 4, with height 2.

Solved ExampleThird-Quadrant Conversion
Fully solved — step by step

Convert $w = -3 - 4j$
to polar form.

Step 1 · Identify Cartesian parts

$w = x + jy$ with $x = -3$ and $y = -4$ ($j=\sqrt{-1}$). Both negative: $w$ is in the third quadrant.

Step 2 · Compute the modulus
$$r \;=\; \sqrt{(-3)^{2} + (-4)^{2}} \;=\; \sqrt{25} \;=\; 5$$
Step 3 · Compute the angle
$$\arctan\!\left(\tfrac{-4}{-3}\right) = \arctan\!\left(\tfrac{4}{3}\right) = 0.9273 \text{ rad}$$ $$\theta = 0.9273 + \pi = 4.0689 \text{ rad} \approx 4.07 \text{ rad}$$

$x < 0$, so add $\pi$ (the two minus signs cancelled in the ratio).

Result
$$w = (5,\; 4.07\text{ rad}) \approx (5,\; 233.1^{\circ})$$

Check: $5\cos 4.0689=-3$ and $5\sin 4.0689=-4$.

FIG. · $w = -3-4j$ in the third quadrant. The $\pi$ correction is essential.

Solved ExampleAdding Two Complex Numbers
Fully solved — step by step

Find $z_a + z_b$
and its polar form.

$z_a$
$|z_a|=3,\quad \angle z_a = \tfrac{\pi}{2}$
$z_b$
$\mathrm{Re}\{z_b\}=4,\quad \mathrm{Im}\{z_b\}=0$
Step 1 · Convert the polar number to Cartesian
$$\mathrm{Re}\{z_a\} = 3\cos\tfrac{\pi}{2} = 3\cdot 0 = 0 \qquad \mathrm{Im}\{z_a\} = 3\sin\tfrac{\pi}{2} = 3\cdot 1 = 3$$

$z_a = 0 + 3j$ — purely imaginary, pointing straight up.

Step 2 · Add in Cartesian (always add in Cartesian!)
$$z_a + z_b \;=\; (0 + 4) + (3 + 0)j \;=\; 4 + 3j$$
Step 3 · Convert sum to polar
$$r = \sqrt{4^{2}+3^{2}} = \sqrt{16+9} = 5 \qquad \theta = \arctan\tfrac{3}{4} = 0.6435 \text{ rad} = 36.9^{\circ}$$

$x=4>0$, so no $\pi$ correction. Note $|z_a+z_b|=5\neq 3+4=|z_a|+|z_b|$: moduli do not add.

FIG. · Parallelogram rule: $z_a + z_b = 4 + 3j$, modulus 5.

Solutionz₃ and z₄
Solution

Use the symmetries.

Third number · Cartesian to polar
$$|z_3| = \sqrt{5^{2} + (-2)^{2}} = \sqrt{29} \approx 5.385$$ $$\theta_3 = \arctan\!\left(\tfrac{-2}{5}\right) = \arctan(-0.4) = -0.3805 \text{ rad} = -21.8^{\circ}$$

$x=5>0$: no $\pi$ correction.

Fourth number · Polar to Cartesian
$$\mathrm{Re}\{z_4\} = 4\cos\!\left(-\tfrac{\pi}{6}\right) = 4\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} = 2\sqrt{3} \approx 3.464$$ $$\mathrm{Im}\{z_4\} = 4\sin\!\left(-\tfrac{\pi}{6}\right) = 4\cdot\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\right) = -2$$

$\cos$ is even, $\sin$ is odd.

Symmetry check

$z_3=-z_1$: same modulus $\sqrt{29}$, angle $\theta_1-\pi = 2.7611-3.1416=-0.3805$ ✓. And $z_4=\overline{z_2}$ (its conjugate: same real part, imaginary part negated) has the same modulus 4, angle $-\pi/6$ ✓.

ConceptComplex Numbers as Vectors
Vector interpretation

Addition and scaling
obey 2-D vector rules.

Because a complex number is a point on a plane, scaling $cz$ (real $c$) and the sum $z_1+z_2$ mean exactly what they mean for 2-D vectors.

Practical rule

Add real parts and imaginary parts separately, with $z_k=x_k+jy_k$:

$$z_1+z_2=(x_1+x_2)+j\,(y_1+y_2)$$ $$cz=cx+j\,cy$$

PitfallsCommon Mistakes
Common mistakes · ≈ 2 min

Where the sign goes wrong.

  1. Forgetting $\pi$ for $x<0$: the bare $\arctan(y/x)$ only knows slope, not which side of the origin.
  2. Adding polar forms. $|z_1+z_2|\neq|z_1|+|z_2|$ (Example B gave $5\neq 7$). Add in Cartesian.
  3. Degrees in a calculator set to radians (or vice versa): this course uses radians.
  4. Mixing range conventions. $w=-3-4j$ is $4.0689$ rad in $[0,2\pi)$ and $-2.2143$ rad in $(-\pi,\pi]$ (what atan2 returns): same direction, since $4.0689-2\pi=-2.2143$.
FIG. · Dashed gray ray: what $\arctan(y/x)$ alone returns ($-21.8^{\circ}$ for $z=-5+2j$, $+53.1^{\circ}$ for $w=-3-4j$). Red arrow: the true direction. Both are off by exactly $\pi$.

Lecture 01Summary
Take with you

Four things to remember.

01 · WHAT
A complex number is a point on $\mathbb{C}$.
Two equivalent addresses: $(x,y)$ or $(r,\theta)$.
02 · POLAR → CARTESIAN
$x = r\cos\theta,\;\; y = r\sin\theta$
Sign is automatic — $\sin$ and $\cos$ handle the quadrant.
03 · CARTESIAN → POLAR
$r = \sqrt{x^2+y^2},\;\; \theta = \arctan(y/x)$
Add $\pi$ if $x<0$. Use $\mathrm{atan2}$ in code.
04 · ARITHMETIC
Addition and scaling follow 2-D vector rules.
Add in Cartesian. Multiplication comes next lecture.
