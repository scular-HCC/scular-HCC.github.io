ConceptMatrices and Vectors
Vocabulary before we multiply

Size, entries, columns, vectors.

Matrix

$A$ is $m\times n$: $m$ rows, $n$ columns. Entry in row $i$, column $j$: $a_{ij}$. Column $j$ as a vector: $\mathbf{a}^{(j)}$.

$$A=\begin{bmatrix}3&1&-1\\2&-1&5\end{bmatrix}\;\;(2\times3)$$ $$a_{12}=1,\quad a_{23}=5,\quad \mathbf{a}^{(2)}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\end{bmatrix}$$
Vector

An $n$-vector $\mathbf{x}$ is an $n\times1$ column with entries $x_1,\dots,x_n$. The unit vector $\mathbf{e}^{(j)}$ has a $1$ in position $j$ and $0$ elsewhere.

$$\mathbf{e}^{(2)}\ (\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{3})=\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{bmatrix}$$
Sum and scaling (entry by entry; sizes must match for a sum)
$$(A+B)_{ij}=a_{ij}+b_{ij},\qquad (cA)_{ij}=c\,a_{ij}$$

ConceptMatrix–Vector Product
Matrix–vector product — two equivalent views

Rows dot $\mathbf{x}$, or $\mathbf{x}$ scales columns.

$A$ is $m\times n$ and $\mathbf{x}$ is an $n$-vector (columns of $A$ = entries of $\mathbf{x}$). The result $\mathbf{y}=A\mathbf{x}$ is an $m$-vector.

View A · Row dot product
$$y_i \,=\, \sum_{j=1}^{n} a_{ij}\, x_j,\qquad i=1,\dots,m$$

The $i$-th entry of $\mathbf{y}$ is the dot product of $A$'s $i$-th row with $\mathbf{x}$.

View B · Column combination
$$\mathbf{y} \,=\, x_1\, \mathbf{a}^{(1)} + \cdots + x_n\, \mathbf{a}^{(n)}$$

$\mathbf{y}$ is a linear combination of $A$'s columns, with the entries $x_j$ as the coefficients.

Notation: dot product and $\Sigma$
The dot product of two $n$-vectors is $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=u_1v_1+\cdots+u_nv_n$, and $\sum_{j=1}^{n}$ means "add the terms for $j=1,\dots,n$". Example: $(3,1,-1)\cdot(2,-1,-4)=3(2)+1(-1)+(-1)(-4)=9$.

ConceptLinearity / Superposition
Why matrices are linear systems

$A$ respects scaling and addition.

$$A\!\left(c_1 \mathbf{x}^{(1)} + c_2 \mathbf{x}^{(2)}\right) \,=\, c_1 A \mathbf{x}^{(1)} + c_2 A \mathbf{x}^{(2)}$$

$c_1, c_2$ are scalars and $\mathbf{x}^{(1)}, \mathbf{x}^{(2)}$ are any two $n$-vectors (superscripts label, not powers). An $m\times n$ matrix $A$ defines a linear transformation $\mathbb{R}^{n}\!\to\!\mathbb{R}^{m}$ (all real $n$-vectors $\to$ all real $m$-vectors), also called a linear system: $n$-dim input $\mathbf{x}$ ⟶ $m$-dim output $\mathbf{y} = A\mathbf{x}$.

Why it holds · one entry of the left side
$$\sum_{j=1}^{n} a_{ij}\bigl(c_1 x^{(1)}_j + c_2 x^{(2)}_j\bigr) = c_1\sum_{j=1}^{n} a_{ij}x^{(1)}_j + c_2\sum_{j=1}^{n} a_{ij}x^{(2)}_j = c_1\bigl(A\mathbf{x}^{(1)}\bigr)_i + c_2\bigl(A\mathbf{x}^{(2)}\bigr)_i$$

Step 1 distributes $a_{ij}$ over the sum (ordinary arithmetic); step 2 recognises each sum as an entry of $A\mathbf{x}$.

PracticeUse Linearity
Practice · ≈ 3 min

Given $A\mathbf{u}$ and $A\mathbf{v}$,
find $A(\mathbf{u} - 2\mathbf{v})$.

Setup

$A$ is an unknown $2\times n$ matrix; $\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}$ are two $n$-vectors. All we know is the output of $A$ on each:

$$A\mathbf{u} = \begin{bmatrix}-1\\4\end{bmatrix},\;\; A\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix}5\\-2\end{bmatrix}$$

Don't compute with $A$. Use superposition.

PracticeENES-222 · L07
SolutionBy Superposition
Solution

Distribute, then combine.

Here $c_1 = 1$ and $c_2 = -2$ are the scalars multiplying $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$.

Apply linearity
$$A(\mathbf{u} - 2\mathbf{v}) \,=\, 1\cdot A\mathbf{u} + (-2)\,A\mathbf{v} \,=\, A\mathbf{u} - 2 A\mathbf{v}$$
Substitute
$$= \begin{bmatrix}-1\\4\end{bmatrix} - 2\begin{bmatrix}5\\-2\end{bmatrix}$$
Scale, then subtract entry by entry
$$= \begin{bmatrix}-1\\4\end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix}10\\-4\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}-1-10\\4-(-4)\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}-11\\8\end{bmatrix}$$
Lesson

You never needed $A$ itself. Knowing how $A$ acts on a set of inputs is enough to know how it acts on every linear combination of them.

ConceptBuild $A$ from Its Action
Constructing the matrix

Columns of $A$ are where
the unit vectors $\mathbf{e}^{(j)}$ land.

$$\mathbf{x} = x_1 \mathbf{e}^{(1)} + \cdots + x_n \mathbf{e}^{(n)}$$
$$A\mathbf{x} = x_1\, A(\mathbf{e}^{(1)}) + \cdots + x_n\, A(\mathbf{e}^{(n)})$$

$\mathbf{e}^{(j)}$ is the vector with a $1$ in position $j$ and $0$ elsewhere, so any $\mathbf{x}$ is the combination on the left (its coefficients are its own entries).

Apply linearity (previous slides) to that combination to get the second line. Comparing with $A\mathbf{x}=x_1\mathbf{a}^{(1)}+\cdots+x_n\mathbf{a}^{(n)}$ shows the $j$-th column of $A$ is exactly $A(\mathbf{e}^{(j)})$.

To find the matrix of a transformation, feed it the standard basis and stack the results column-by-column.

Worked example

Stack the images as columns.

A linear map $T:\mathbb{R}^3\to\mathbb{R}^3$ sends the unit vectors to $T(\mathbf{e}^{(1)})=(3,-1,0)$, $T(\mathbf{e}^{(2)})=(-1,5,4)$, $T(\mathbf{e}^{(3)})=(1,-1,1)$. Find its matrix $A$.

ApplicationFitting an Image
Application · scaling matrix

Fit a 36-inch 16:9 image on a 27-inch 4:3 screen.

Scaling by $a$ is the matrix $aI$, where $I$ is the identity (1s on the diagonal, 0s elsewhere), and every point $\mathbf{p}$ goes to $a\mathbf{p}$. Pick the largest $a$ so the image fits without distortion or cropping. A "36-inch" display means the diagonal is $36$ in.

36-inch, 16:9: diagonal $=\sqrt{16^2+9^2}=\sqrt{337}\approx18.358$ (units of the ratio)
$$w_{36}=36\cdot\tfrac{16}{18.358}\approx31.377\ \text{in},\qquad h_{36}=36\cdot\tfrac{9}{18.358}\approx17.649\ \text{in}$$
27-inch, 4:3: diagonal $=\sqrt{4^2+3^2}=5$
$$w_{27}=27\cdot\tfrac45=21.6\ \text{in},\qquad h_{27}=27\cdot\tfrac35=16.2\ \text{in}$$
Each dimension limits $a$
$$a\le\frac{w_{27}}{w_{36}}=\frac{21.6}{31.377}\approx0.6884,\qquad a\le\frac{h_{27}}{h_{36}}=\frac{16.2}{17.649}\approx0.9179$$

ConceptMatrix–Matrix Product
Matrix–matrix product — check the sizes first

Row $i$ of $A$ dots
column $j$ of $B$.

$$(AB)_{ij} \,=\, \sum_{k=1}^{p} a_{ik}\, b_{kj}$$

Inner dimension must match: $A$ is $m\times p$, $B$ is $p\times n$, product is $m\times n$.

Think of it as $n$ matrix-vector products — one per column of $B$.

ConceptENES-222 · L07
Worked ExampleCompute $AB$
Worked example

$A$ is $2\times 3$, $B$ is $3\times 2$.
Product is $2\times 2$.

$$A = \begin{bmatrix}2 & -1 & 4\\ 1 & 0 & -1\end{bmatrix},\;\; B = \begin{bmatrix}1 & -2 \\ 0 & 3 \\ 1 & 5\end{bmatrix}$$

Inner dimensions agree ($3=3$). $(AB)_{ij}$ is row $i$ of $A$ dotted with column $j$ of $B$.

Entry by entry
$$(AB)_{11} = 2(1) + (-1)(0) + 4(1) = 2 + 0 + 4 = 6$$ $$(AB)_{12} = 2(-2) + (-1)(3) + 4(5) = -4 - 3 + 20 = 13$$ $$(AB)_{21} = 1(1) + 0(0) + (-1)(1) = 1 + 0 - 1 = 0$$ $$(AB)_{22} = 1(-2) + 0(3) + (-1)(5) = -2 + 0 - 5 = -7$$ $$AB \,=\, \begin{bmatrix}6 & 13 \\ 0 & -7\end{bmatrix}$$

Worked Example$AB$ Column by Column
Worked example · the same product, a second way

$AB$ one column at a time.

$$A=\begin{bmatrix}2&-1&4\\1&0&-1\end{bmatrix},\;\; B=\begin{bmatrix}1&-2\\0&3\\1&5\end{bmatrix}$$

Column $j$ of $AB$ is $A\,\mathbf{b}^{(j)}$, a matrix–vector product.

Column 1: $\mathbf{b}^{(1)}=(1,0,1)$
$$A\mathbf{b}^{(1)}=1\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix}+0\begin{bmatrix}-1\\0\end{bmatrix}+1\begin{bmatrix}4\\-1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2+0+4\\1+0-1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}6\\0\end{bmatrix}$$
Column 2: $\mathbf{b}^{(2)}=(-2,3,5)$
$$A\mathbf{b}^{(2)}=-2\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix}+3\begin{bmatrix}-1\\0\end{bmatrix}+5\begin{bmatrix}4\\-1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-4-3+20\\-2+0-5\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}13\\-7\end{bmatrix}$$

So $AB=\begin{bmatrix}6&13\\0&-7\end{bmatrix}$, identical to the entry-by-entry result and to the notebook output.

Worked Example$AB\neq BA$
Worked example · order matters

Compute $AB$ and $BA$;
show $AB\neq BA$.

$$A=\begin{bmatrix}2&{-1}\\1&3\end{bmatrix},\;B=\begin{bmatrix}1&4\\0&{-2}\end{bmatrix}$$

Both are $2\times2$, so $AB$ and $BA$ are both defined.

Step 1 · Product AB (rows of A, columns of B)
$$AB=\begin{bmatrix}2(1)+(-1)(0)&2(4)+(-1)(-2)\\1(1)+3(0)&1(4)+3(-2)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2+0&8+2\\1+0&4-6\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2&10\\1&{-2}\end{bmatrix}$$
Step 2 · Product BA (rows of B, columns of A)
$$BA=\begin{bmatrix}1(2)+4(1)&1(-1)+4(3)\\0(2)+(-2)(1)&0(-1)+(-2)(3)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2+4&-1+12\\0-2&0-6\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}6&11\\{-2}&{-6}\end{bmatrix}$$
Non-commutativity

$(AB)_{11}=2\neq 6=(BA)_{11}$, so $AB\neq BA$: order matters.

ConceptCommon Mistakes
Check yourself

Three mistakes to avoid.

1 · Columns dotted with $\mathbf{x}$

$y_i$ uses row $i$ of $A$. For $A=\begin{bmatrix}3&1&-1\\2&-1&5\end{bmatrix}$ the first entry is $3(2)+1(-1)+(-1)(-4)=9$, not $3(2)+2(-1)$.

2 · Sizes that do not match

$AB$ needs (columns of $A$) $=$ (rows of $B$). A $2\times3$ times a $2\times3$ is undefined; a $2\times3$ times a $3\times2$ is $2\times2$.

3 · Assuming $AB=BA$

Even for $2\times2$ matrices the two products usually differ: $(AB)_{11}=2$ but $(BA)_{11}=6$ in our example. Always keep the order.
