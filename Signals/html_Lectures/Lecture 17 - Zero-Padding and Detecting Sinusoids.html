ConceptZero-Padding Pair
Zero-padding

Pad $s$ with $N - L$ zeros
to get a length-$N$ vector $y$.

$$y = [s; 0_{N-L}]$$
$$\text{If } N = ML: \;\; S[k] = Y[kM]$$

Take every $M$-th entry of $Y$ to recover $S$. The other entries are extra — samples of a denser frequency grid.

Inner product with the $kM$-th Fourier sinusoid of length $ML$ matches inner product with the $k$-th Fourier sinusoid of length $L$ — because $s$ is nonzero only on indices $0\!:\!L-1$.

Worked Example$L=3$, $N=12$
Worked example

Two extracted DFTs.

Setup
$$y = (a, b, c, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0)^{\top}$$ $$Y = (Y_0, Y_1, \ldots, Y_{11})^{\top}$$
Length-3 DFT
$$(a, b, c)^{\top} \,\leftrightarrow\, (Y_0,\, Y_4,\, Y_8)^{\top}$$
Length-4 DFT
$$(a, b, c, 0)^{\top} \,\leftrightarrow\, (Y_0,\, Y_3,\, Y_6,\, Y_9)^{\top}$$

Each "sub-DFT" lives at a stride determined by the ratio $N/L$.

ConceptSpectral Interpolation
Why zero-pad

More $N$ ⇒ denser frequency samples.

$$\omega_k \,=\, k\,\tfrac{2\pi}{N}$$

Zero-padding does not add information — it interpolates the existing spectrum onto a finer grid.

For $N \gg L$, the amplitude spectrum looks almost continuous when plotted against $\omega$.

Use it to look at the spectrum, not to gain resolution from new data.

ConceptDetecting a Sinusoid
Detecting a sinusoid

Peak in $|S|$ ⇒ frequency of $s$.

Sample a continuous sinusoid: $s[n] = A\cos(\omega_0 n + \phi) + \epsilon[n]$.

  • The two prominent peaks of $|S|$ sit at indices $k_0$ and $L - k_0$ — the symmetric DT frequency pair.
  • $A$ and $\phi$ follow from the amplitude and phase at the peak.
  • Noise scatters into a flat floor; if SNR is decent, the peaks stick out.

Aliasing concern: $\omega_0$ must be in $[0, \pi]$ for the sampled signal to faithfully represent the continuous one (L06).

ConceptSpectral Mainlobe
When $\omega_0$ is not a Fourier frequency

The DFT samples a continuous mainlobe.

$$\langle v(\omega), s\rangle \,=\, \sum_{n=0}^{L-1} e^{j(\omega_0 - \omega)n} \,=\, e^{-j(L-1)(\omega-\omega_0)/2}\,\frac{\sin(L(\omega-\omega_0)/2)}{\sin((\omega-\omega_0)/2)}$$
Modulus
$$|\langle v(\omega),s\rangle| \,=\, \left|\frac{\sin(L(\omega-\omega_0)/2)}{\sin((\omega-\omega_0)/2)}\right|$$

Max value $L$, reached at $\omega = \omega_0$.

Width of the mainlobe shrinks like $\sim 2\pi/L$ — longer sample, sharper peak.

This is the Dirichlet kernel in disguise.

PracticeLocate $\omega_0$
Practice

Detect $\omega_0$ from samples of
a complex sinusoid.

Setup
$$s[n] = e^{j(0.317)(2\pi)\,n},\;\; n = 0, 1, \ldots, 19,\;\; L = 20$$
  • Compute the length-$L$ DFT. Where is the peak?
  • Zero-pad to $N = 1000$. Where is the peak now?
  • Compare the two peak positions to $\omega_0/2\pi$.
PracticeENES-222 · L17
SolutionBetter Resolution from Padding
Solution

Padding sharpens the read-out.

Length-20 DFT

Fourier frequencies $f_k = k/20$ for $k = 0, \ldots, 19$. The nearest to $0.317$ is $6/20 = 0.30$ (or $7/20 = 0.35$). The peak appears at $k = 6$ — off by $0.017$.

Zero-padded to 1000

Now $f_k = k/1000$. The peak lands at $k = 317$ — i.e. $f = 0.317$, the exact $\omega_0/2\pi$.

Why

The length-20 DFT samples the continuous mainlobe at 20 points, missing the peak. Zero-padding samples it at 1000 points — close enough to find the maximum.

Mainlobe width is fixed by $L = 20$ — padding doesn't narrow it, just locates the maximum more precisely.

Solved Example A · §3.7.3

Zero-pad $\mathbf{s}=(1,1,1)^\top$
to $N=12$.

Find which entries of $Y$ equal $S[k]$.

Step 1 · DFT of $\mathbf{s}$ ($L=3$)
$S[0]=3,\;S[1]=0,\;S[2]=0$
Step 2 · Zero-pad: $N=12=4L$, so $M=4$
$$Y[kM]=Y[4k]=S[k]$$ $Y[0]=3,\;Y[4]=0,\;Y[8]=0$; other entries interpolate.
Key point

Zero-padding interpolates the spectrum; it adds no new frequency information.

Solved Example B · §3.8.2

Detect $\omega_0$ from a short
sinusoidal vector.

$s[n]=\cos(0.7\pi n)$, $n=0{:}7$ ($L=8$). Zero-pad to $N=32$.

Step 1 · True peak bin
$k_0=\omega_0 N/(2\pi)=0.7\pi\cdot32/(2\pi)=11.2$ (non-integer)
Step 2 · Nearest bin
Closest Fourier frequency: $k=11$ ($\omega=0.6875\pi$). $$\hat\omega_0=11\cdot2\pi/32\approx0.6875\pi$$
Resolution

$\Delta\omega=2\pi/N=\pi/16$. Larger $N$ $\Rightarrow$ finer resolution.
