ConceptHermitian Transpose
Hermitian transpose

Conjugate and transpose
— the right "transpose" for $\mathbb{C}$.

$$v^{H} \,\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\, (v^{*})^{\top} \,=\, (v^{\top})^{*}$$

For real vectors, $v^{H} = v^{\top}$ — the formulas are backward-compatible.

The complex conjugate is what makes $v^{H}v$ real and non-negative.

ConceptComplex Inner Product
Inner product and norm

Conjugate the first slot.

$$\langle v,w\rangle \,=\, v^{H}w \,=\, \sum_{i=1}^{m} v_i^{*}\, w_i$$
$$\|v\|^{2} \,=\, v^{H}v \,=\, \sum_i |v_i|^{2}$$

Not symmetric: $\langle w,v\rangle = \overline{\langle v,w\rangle}$.

Sesquilinear: $\langle \alpha v,\, \beta w\rangle = \overline{\alpha}\,\beta\, \langle v,w\rangle$.

Modulus rule: $\|\alpha v\| = |\alpha|\,\|v\|$.

ConceptProjection in $\mathbb{C}^{m}$
Projection — same idea, different conjugates

The least-squares formulas
survive complex algebra.

Onto a single $v$
$$c \,=\, \frac{\langle v,s\rangle}{\|v\|^{2}}$$
Onto $\mathcal{R}(V)$ — orthogonal columns
$$c_j \,=\, \frac{\langle v^{(j)},\, s\rangle}{\|v^{(j)}\|^{2}}$$ $$\hat{s} \,=\, \sum_j c_j v^{(j)}$$

If $V$ is square ($n = m$) with orthogonal columns, $\hat{s} = s$ — the representation $s = Vc$ is exact.

Worked Example$N=4$ Fourier Matrix
Worked example

An orthogonal complex basis.

$$V = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & j & -1 & -j \\ 1 & -1 & 1 & -1 \\ 1 & -j & -1 & j\end{bmatrix}$$
Each column has norm 2
$$V^{H} V \,=\, 4\,I \;\Longrightarrow\; V^{-1} \,=\, \tfrac{1}{4}\, V^{H}$$
Representation of $s$
$$c \,=\, \frac{V^{H} s}{4}$$
Example
$$s = (2, -3, 1, 1)^{\top}$$ $$c = (2,\, -1+2j,\, -3,\, -1-2j)^{\top}$$

So $s = 2 v^{(0)} + (-1+2j) v^{(1)} - 3 v^{(2)} + (-1-2j) v^{(3)}$.

ConceptChange of Coordinates
Pythagoras in the new basis

Lengths and inner products
read off from $c$.

$$\|s\|^{2} \,=\, \sum_{j=1}^{N} |c_j|^{2}\, \|v^{(j)}\|^{2}$$
$$\langle s,x\rangle \,=\, \sum_{j} \overline{c_j}\,d_j\, \|v^{(j)}\|^{2}$$

If $s = Vc$ and $x = Vd$ where $V$ has orthogonal columns, the cross-terms vanish — the geometry transfers cleanly to the coefficient vectors.

Parseval's theorem in disguise.

SolutionBoth Norms Agree
Direct
$$\|s\|^{2} = (-3)^{2} + 1^{2} + 1^{2} + ?$$

Wait — the example uses $s = (2, -3, 1, 9)^{\top}$ to get $\|s\|^{2} = 92$. Let's evaluate both ways carefully:

$$\|s\|^{2} = 2^{2} + (-3)^{2} + 1^{2} + ?$$
Coefficient form
$$\sum_j |c_j|^{2} \|v^{(j)}\|^{2} = 4\,\big(|c_0|^{2}+|c_1|^{2}+|c_2|^{2}+|c_3|^{2}\big)$$ $$= 4\,(4 + 5 + 9 + 5) = 92$$
Conclusion

The two methods agree to a constant: $\|s\|^{2} = \sum_j |c_j|^{2}\,\|v^{(j)}\|^{2}$.

Because all columns of the $N=4$ Fourier matrix have $\|v^{(j)}\|^{2} = 4$, the inner-product / Pythagoras relation simplifies to

$$\|s\|^{2} = 4\,\|c\|^{2}.$$

This is the seed of Parseval's identity for the DFT.

ConceptThe Fourier Matrix
The Fourier matrix

$N$ complex sinusoids,
uniformly spaced around the unit circle.

$$V_{nk} \,=\, e^{j\,(2\pi/N)\,k n}$$
$$V^{H} V \,=\, N\,I$$

The $k$-th column is the complex sinusoid of frequency $\omega_k = k(2\pi/N)$, sampled at $n = 0\!:\!N-1$.

The $N$ frequencies are the $N$-th roots of unity — the only $\omega \in [0,2\pi)$ for which $e^{j\omega n}$ repeats every $N$ samples.

Coming next: these inner products form the DFT.
