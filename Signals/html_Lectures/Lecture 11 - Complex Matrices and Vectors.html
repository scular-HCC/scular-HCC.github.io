MotivationWhy the Transpose Fails
Motivation · ≈ 2 min

Try $v^{\top}v$ on complex vectors, with $j^{2}=-1$.

Transpose only
$$v=(1,\,j)^{\top}:\;\;v^{\top}v=1^{2}+j^{2}=0$$$$v=(1+j,\,2-j)^{\top}:$$$$v^{\top}v=(1+j)^{2}+(2-j)^{2}=2j+(3-4j)=3-2j$$

Zero for a nonzero vector, or complex: neither is a length.

Conjugate first

Conjugate: $(a+jb)^{*}=a-jb$, so $z^{*}z=a^{2}+b^{2}=|z|^{2}\ge0$.

$$|1|^{2}+|j|^{2}=1+1=2$$$$|1+j|^{2}+|2-j|^{2}=2+5=7$$

Real, positive, and equal to the squared length.

MotivationENES-222 · L11
ConceptHermitian Transpose
Hermitian transpose

Conjugate and transpose
— the right "transpose" for $\mathbb{C}$.

$$v^{H} \,\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\, (v^{*})^{\top} \,=\, (v^{\top})^{*}$$

Here $x^{*}$ is the complex conjugate: $(a+jb)^{*}=a-jb$, with $j^2=-1$. On a vector, conjugate every entry.

For real vectors, $v^{H} = v^{\top}$, so the formulas are backward-compatible.

Conjugating makes $v_i^{*}v_i=|v_i|^2\ge 0$, so $v^{H}v$ is real and non-negative.

Worked ExampleHermitian Transpose by Hand
Worked example · ≈ 3 min

Conjugate every entry and transpose.

1 · A vector

$v=(1+j,\;2-j,\;3j)^{\top}$

$$v^{*}=(1-j,\;2+j,\;-3j)^{\top}$$$$v^{H}=\big[\,1-j\;\;\;2+j\;\;\;-3j\,\big]$$

Column becomes row, each entry conjugated.

2 · A matrix

$A=\begin{bmatrix}1&2j\\3-j&4\end{bmatrix}$. Transpose, then conjugate:

$$A^{\top}=\begin{bmatrix}1&3-j\\2j&4\end{bmatrix}\;\Rightarrow\;A^{H}=\begin{bmatrix}1&3+j\\-2j&4\end{bmatrix}$$

Check: $(A^{H})^{H}=A$. Real entries are unchanged.

ConceptComplex Inner Product
Inner product and norm

Conjugate the first slot.

$$\langle v,w\rangle \,=\, v^{H}w \,=\, \sum_{i=1}^{m} v_i^{*}\, w_i$$
$$\|v\|^{2} \,=\, v^{H}v \,=\, \sum_i |v_i|^{2}$$

Not symmetric: $\langle w,v\rangle = \sum_i w_i^{*}v_i = \overline{\sum_i v_i^{*}w_i} = \overline{\langle v,w\rangle}$.

Sesquilinear: $\langle \alpha v,\, \beta w\rangle = \sum_i (\alpha v_i)^{*}\beta w_i = \overline{\alpha}\,\beta\, \langle v,w\rangle$.

Modulus rule: $\|\alpha v\|^2 = \overline{\alpha}\alpha\|v\|^2 = |\alpha|^2\|v\|^2$, so $\|\alpha v\| = |\alpha|\,\|v\|$.

Worked ExampleOrder and Scalars, with Numbers
Worked example · ≈ 3 min

Check the rules with $v=(1+j,\,2-j)$, $w=(j,\,1+2j)$.

From Solved Example A (slide 26): $\langle v,w\rangle=1+6j$.

Order matters
$$\langle w,v\rangle=(-j)(1+j)+(1-2j)(2-j)$$$$=(1-j)+(-5j)=1-6j$$$$=\overline{1+6j}=\overline{\langle v,w\rangle}\;\checkmark$$
Scalars: conjugated in the first slot only
$$\langle jv,w\rangle=\bar{j}\,\langle v,w\rangle=-j(1+6j)=6-j$$$$\langle v,jw\rangle=j\,\langle v,w\rangle=j(1+6j)=-6+j$$

Direct: $jv=(-1+j,\,1+2j)$, $\langle jv,w\rangle=(-1-j)j+(1-2j)(1+2j)=(1-j)+5=6-j$.

And $\|jv\|^{2}=|j|^{2}\|v\|^{2}=7$. Wrong rule: $v^{\top}w=(1+j)j+(2-j)(1+2j)=3+4j\ne1+6j$.

ConceptProjection in $\mathbb{C}^{m}$
Projection — same idea, different conjugates

The least-squares formulas
survive complex algebra.

Onto a single $v$
$$c \,=\, \frac{\langle v,s\rangle}{\|v\|^{2}}$$
Onto $\mathcal{R}(V)$ — orthogonal columns
$$c_j \,=\, \frac{\langle v^{(j)},\, s\rangle}{\|v^{(j)}\|^{2}}$$ $$\hat{s} \,=\, \sum_j c_j v^{(j)}$$

If $V$ is square ($n = m$) with orthogonal columns, $\hat{s} = s$ — the representation $s = Vc$ is exact.

Worked ExampleBuilding the N = 4 Matrix by Hand
Worked example · ≈ 3 min

Entry $(n,k)$ is $j^{kn}$: reduce the exponent mod 4.

Powers of $j$: $j^{0}=1,\;j^{1}=j,\;j^{2}=-1,\;j^{3}=-j,\;j^{4}=1$, so only $kn \bmod 4$ matters.

$a \bmod 4$ is the remainder when $a$ is divided by 4, one of $0,1,2,3$: $6 \bmod 4=2$ (since $6=1\cdot4+2$) and $9 \bmod 4=1$.

1 · The exponent products, then reduced mod 4
$$\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0&0\\0&1&2&3\\0&2&4&6\\0&3&6&9\end{bmatrix}\;\xrightarrow{\bmod 4}\;\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0&0\\0&1&2&3\\0&2&0&2\\0&3&2&1\end{bmatrix}$$
2 · Replace each exponent by a power of j
$$\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1&1\\1&j&-1&-j\\1&-1&1&-1\\1&-j&-1&j\end{bmatrix}$$

Example $(n,k)=(3,3)$: $kn=9=2\cdot4+1$, so $j^{9}=j$. The matrix is symmetric because $kn=nk$.

Worked Example$N=4$ Fourier Matrix
Worked example · ≈ 4 min

An orthogonal complex basis.

$$V = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & j & -1 & -j \\ 1 & -1 & 1 & -1 \\ 1 & -j & -1 & j\end{bmatrix}$$

Rows $n$ and columns $k$ are numbered $0$ to $3$. The entry is $V_{nk}=e^{j(2\pi/4)kn}=j^{kn}$, with $j^2=-1$, $j^3=-j$, $j^4=1$. Column $k$ is called $v^{(k)}$.

Columns are orthogonal, norm 2
$$\|v^{(k)}\|^{2} = 1+1+1+1 = 4$$ $$\langle v^{(k)},v^{(l)}\rangle = 0\;\;(k\ne l)$$ $$V^{H} V \,=\, 4\,I \;\Longrightarrow\; V^{-1} \,=\, \tfrac{1}{4}\, V^{H}$$

Entry $(k,l)$ of $V^HV$ is $\langle v^{(k)},v^{(l)}\rangle$. All six pairs are checked in Solved Example B. $I$ is the identity matrix (1 on the diagonal, 0 elsewhere), so $V^HV=4I$ has 4 on the diagonal and 0 off it.

Representation of s
$$s = Vc \;\Rightarrow\; V^{H}s = V^{H}Vc = 4c \;\Rightarrow\; c = \frac{V^{H} s}{4}$$

ConceptChange of Coordinates
Pythagoras in the new basis

Lengths and inner products read off from $c$.

$$\|s\|^{2} \,=\, \sum_{j=0}^{N-1} |c_j|^{2}\, \|v^{(j)}\|^{2}$$ $$\langle s,x\rangle \,=\, \sum_{j} \overline{c_j}\,d_j\, \|v^{(j)}\|^{2}$$

Take $s = Vc$ and $x = Vd$, where $V$ has orthogonal columns. Then the geometry transfers cleanly to the coefficient vectors.

Parseval's theorem in disguise.

Why the cross-terms vanish
$$\langle s,x\rangle=\Big\langle \sum_i c_iv^{(i)},\sum_j d_jv^{(j)}\Big\rangle=\sum_i\sum_j \overline{c_i}\,d_j\,\langle v^{(i)},v^{(j)}\rangle=\sum_j \overline{c_j}\,d_j\,\|v^{(j)}\|^{2}$$

Sesquilinearity expands the double sum; only $i=j$ survives, and $x=s$ ($d=c$) gives the norm formula.

SolutionBoth Norms Agree
Direct
$$\|s\|^{2} = (-3)^{2} + 1^{2} + 1^{2} + 9^{2} = 9+1+1+81 = 92$$
Each coefficient squared

Use $|a+jb|^2=a^2+b^2$.

$$|c_0|^2=4,\quad |c_1|^2=1+4=5$$ $$|c_2|^2=9,\quad |c_3|^2=1+4=5$$
Coefficient form
$$\sum_j |c_j|^{2}\|v^{(j)}\|^{2}=4\,(4+5+9+5)=4\cdot23=92$$
Conclusion

The two methods agree: $\|s\|^{2} = \sum_j |c_j|^{2}\,\|v^{(j)}\|^{2} = 92$.

Because all columns of the $N=4$ Fourier matrix have $\|v^{(j)}\|^{2} = 4$, the relation simplifies to

$$\|s\|^{2} = 4\,\|c\|^{2}, \quad \|c\|^2=23.$$

This is the seed of Parseval's identity for the DFT.

ConceptThe Fourier Matrix
The Fourier matrix

$N$ complex sinusoids,
uniformly spaced around the unit circle.

$$V_{nk} \,=\, e^{j\,(2\pi/N)\,k n}$$
$$V^{H} V \,=\, N\,I$$

The $k$-th column is the complex sinusoid of frequency $\Omega_k = k(2\pi/N)$, sampled at $n = 0\!:\!N-1$. For $N=4$ this is $j^{kn}$, the matrix on the earlier slides.

The $N$ frequencies are the $N$-th roots of unity: the only $\Omega \in [0,2\pi)$ for which $e^{j\Omega n}$ repeats every $N$ samples.

Coming next: these inner products form the DFT.

Common MistakesFour Traps
Common mistakes · ≈ 2 min

Four traps, each with a number that exposes it.

1 · Using the transpose

$v=(1,j)$: $v^{\top}v=0$ but $v^{H}v=2$. For the vectors of slide 26: $v^{\top}w=3+4j$, the correct $\langle v,w\rangle=1+6j$.

2 · Assuming symmetry

$\langle v,w\rangle=1+6j$ but $\langle w,v\rangle=1-6j$: swapping the order conjugates the result.

3 · Conjugating the wrong scalar

Only the first slot conjugates: $\langle jv,w\rangle=6-j$ but $\langle v,jw\rangle=-6+j$.

4 · Using V in place of its conjugate

For $s=(-3,1,1,9)$: the row $(1,j,-1,-j)$ gives $(-4-8j)/4=-1-2j$, which is $c_3$, not $c_1=-1+2j$. Conjugate the rows.

Solved Example A · ≈ 3 min

Compute $\|\mathbf{v}\|$ and $\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\rangle$ for complex vectors.

$\mathbf{v}=(1+j,\,2-j)^\top$, $\mathbf{w}=(j,\,1+2j)^\top$, with $j^2=-1$.

Step 1 · Norm
$$|1+j|^2=1^2+1^2=2,\quad|2-j|^2=2^2+(-1)^2=5$$ $$\|\mathbf{v}\|^2=2+5=7,\quad\|\mathbf{v}\|=\sqrt{7}$$
Step 2 · Hermitian inner product

$\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\rangle=\mathbf{v}^H\mathbf{w}=v_1^*w_1+v_2^*w_2$ (conjugate $\mathbf{v}$ only).

$$v_1^*w_1=(1-j)(j)=j-j^2=j+1$$ $$v_2^*w_2=(2+j)(1+2j)=2+4j+j+2j^2=2+5j-2=5j$$ $$\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\rangle=(1+j)+5j=1+6j$$

Solved Example B · ≈ 4 min

Verify $V^HV=4I$ for the $N=4$ Fourier matrix.

Step 1 · Entry (k, l) of V^H V

$\overline{j^{kn}}=j^{-kn}$ since $|j|=1$. Let $r=j^{\,l-k}$:

$$\langle v^{(k)},v^{(l)}\rangle=\sum_{n=0}^{3}j^{-kn}j^{ln}=\sum_{n=0}^{3}r^{n}$$
Step 2 · Diagonal: k = l
$$r=1\Rightarrow\sum_{n=0}^{3}1=4=N\;\checkmark$$

E.g. $v^{(1)}=(1,j,-1,-j)$: $1+1+1+1=4$.

Step 3 · Off the diagonal: k not equal l
$$r\in\{j,-1,-j\},\; r^4=1,\; r\ne1\;\Rightarrow\;\sum_{n=0}^{3}r^n=\frac{1-r^4}{1-r}=0\;\checkmark\quad(\text{geometric sum})$$ $$\langle v^{(0)},v^{(1)}\rangle=1+j+(-1)+(-j)=0$$ $$\langle v^{(1)},v^{(2)}\rangle=1(1)+(-j)(-1)+(-1)(1)+(j)(-1)=1+j-1-j=0$$
