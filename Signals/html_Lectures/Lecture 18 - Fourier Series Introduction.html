ConceptPeriodic Continuous-Time Signals
Periodicity

$s(t + T_0) = s(t)$ for all $t$.

$$T_0 \;\;\to\;\; f_0 = \tfrac{1}{T_0}\;\text{(Hz)},\;\; \Omega_0 = \tfrac{2\pi}{T_0}\;\text{(rad/s)}$$

The fundamental period is the smallest $T_0 > 0$ for which the identity holds.

If $s$ has period $T_0$, it also has period $mT_0$ — useful for picking common periods of sums.

ConceptENES-222 · L18
ConceptFourier Sinusoids & Harmonics
Harmonics

$v^{(k)}(t) = e^{j k \Omega_0 t}$ for $k \in \mathbb{Z}$.

$$\Omega_k \,=\, k\,\Omega_0$$

The positive multiples $2\Omega_0, 3\Omega_0, \ldots$ are the harmonics of $\Omega_0$.

These are the only complex sinusoids $e^{j\Omega t}$ periodic with period $T_0$.

Compare with the discrete case (L04, L11): both have a discrete set of "allowed" frequencies, but here the set is infinite.

PracticeHarmonically Related?
Practice

$f_1 = 50$ Hz, $f_2 = 87.5$ Hz.

  • Are $f_1$ and $f_2$ harmonics of a common $f_0$?
  • If yes, what is the largest such $f_0$?
  • What other values of $f_0$ would also work?
PracticeENES-222 · L18
Solution$f_0 = 12.5$ Hz
Ratio test
$$\tfrac{f_2}{f_1} = \tfrac{87.5}{50} = \tfrac{7}{4} \in \mathbb{Q}\;\checkmark$$

Rational ⇒ harmonically related.

Largest $f_0$

$f_0$ must divide both $50$ and $87.5$. The greatest such value (with rational arithmetic) is $\gcd(50, 87.5) = 12.5$ Hz.

$$f_1 = 4 f_0,\;\; f_2 = 7 f_0$$
Other valid $f_0$'s

Any submultiple of $12.5$ Hz also works: $6.25$, $4.166\overline{6}$, $3.125$, etc. — i.e. $f_0 = 12.5/m$ for any positive integer $m$.

$12.5$ Hz is the largest — and corresponds to the fundamental. The others over-count harmonics that don't actually appear.

ConceptThe Complex Fourier Series
Synthesis equation

Periodic $s(t)$ as a sum
of harmonics.

$$s(t) \,=\, \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} S_k\, e^{j k \Omega_0 t}$$
Real-valued $s$
$$S_{-k} \,=\, S_{k}^{*} \;\;\text{for all } k \in \mathbb{Z}$$

Same symmetry as the DFT of a real signal.

Convergence

Infinite sum — may not converge for all $s$. We'll work with signals for which it does.

Worked ExampleBandlimited Series
Worked example

Three cosines
and a DC offset.

With $f_0 = 12.5$ Hz, $T_0 = 0.08$ s:

$$s(t) \,=\, 5.0 \,+\, 6.2\cos(25\pi t + 0.3)$$ $$\,+\, 4.6\cos(100\pi t - 1.9) \,+\, 2.8\cos(175\pi t + 2.5)$$
Convert each cosine to two exponentials
$$A\cos(\Omega t + \phi) = \tfrac{A}{2}\,e^{j\phi}\,e^{j\Omega t} + \tfrac{A}{2}\,e^{-j\phi}\,e^{-j\Omega t}$$
Nonzero coefficients
$$S_0 = 5.0$$ $$S_{\pm 1} = 3.1\,e^{\pm j 0.3}$$ $$S_{\pm 4} = 2.3\,e^{\mp j 1.9}$$ $$S_{\pm 7} = 1.4\,e^{\pm j 2.5}$$

ConceptAn Infinite Series
Sawtooth wave

Infinitely many harmonics —
and Gibbs ringing at the jumps.

$$s(t) \,=\, \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\, \tfrac{2(-1)^{k+1}}{\pi k}\,\sin(k\Omega_0 t)$$

Truncating to $K$ terms approximates the sawtooth $s(t) = 2t/T_0$ on $(-T_0/2, T_0/2)$.

Near each discontinuity the partial sums overshoot by ~$9\%$ — the Gibbs phenomenon. More terms narrow the ripple but do not eliminate the overshoot.

Take with you

Four facts.

01 · HARMONICS
$\Omega_k = k\Omega_0$
Integer multiples of $\Omega_0 = 2\pi/T_0$.
02 · SYNTHESIS
$s(t) = \sum_k S_k e^{j k \Omega_0 t}$
Infinite sum — convergence required.

$S_{-k} = S_{k}^{*}$
Same symmetry as the DFT.
04 · GIBBS
Overshoot near discontinuities.
Doesn't vanish as $K \to \infty$.
