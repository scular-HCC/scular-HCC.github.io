Fourier Series
Periodic signals in continuous time are sums of harmonically related sinusoids. The DFT idea, lifted to the integral.

ConceptPeriodic Continuous-Time Signals
Periodicity

$s(t + T_0) = s(t)$ for all $t$.

$$T_0 \;\;\to\;\; f_0 = \tfrac{1}{T_0}\;\text{(Hz)},\;\; \omega_0 = \tfrac{2\pi}{T_0}\;\text{(rad/s)}$$

The fundamental period is the smallest $T_0 > 0$ for which the identity holds.

If $s$ has period $T_0$, it also has period $mT_0$ — useful for picking common periods of sums.

ConceptENES-222 · L18
Worked ExamplePeriod of a Sum of Sinusoids
Worked example · is the sum periodic?

Find $T_0$ for a sum.

Given
$$s(t)=\cos(2\pi\cdot6\,t)+\cos(2\pi\cdot9\,t)$$
Step 1 · frequencies and ratio
$f_1=6$ Hz, $f_2=9$ Hz. $\tfrac{f_2}{f_1}=\tfrac96=\tfrac32$, a ratio of integers, so a common period exists.
Step 2 · largest common frequency
$f_0=\gcd(6,9)=3$ Hz, so $$T_0=\tfrac{1}{f_0}=\tfrac13\text{ s},\qquad\omega_0=2\pi f_0=6\pi\text{ rad/s}$$
Step 3 · harmonic numbers
$6=2\cdot3$ and $9=3\cdot3$: the two cosines are the 2nd and 3rd harmonics of $f_0$.
Step 4 · check
Periods of the parts: $T_1=\tfrac16$ s and $T_2=\tfrac19$ s. And $T_0=\tfrac13=2T_1=3T_2$ ✓, an integer number of cycles of each.
Counter-example

$\cos(2\pi t)+\cos(2\pi\sqrt2\,t)$: the ratio $\sqrt2$ is irrational, no integers $a,b$ give $a\cdot1=b\sqrt2$, so the sum is not periodic.

Notation: gcd
$\gcd(a,b)$ is the largest number that fits into both $a$ and $b$ a whole number of times: $\gcd(6,9)=3$, since $6=2\cdot3$ and $9=3\cdot3$. It also works for decimals: $\gcd(50,87.5)=12.5$.

ConceptFourier Sinusoids & Harmonics
Harmonics

$v^{(k)}(t) = e^{j k \omega_0 t}$ for $k \in \mathbb{Z}$.

$$\omega_k \,=\, k\,\omega_0,\qquad \omega_0 = \tfrac{2\pi}{T_0}$$

The positive multiples $2\omega_0, 3\omega_0, \ldots$ are the harmonics of $\omega_0$.

Compare with the discrete case (L04, L11): both have a discrete set of "allowed" frequencies, but here the set is infinite.

Why only these frequencies

$e^{j\omega t}$ has period $T_0$ when $e^{j\omega (t+T_0)} = e^{j\omega t}$ for all $t$. Cancel $e^{j\omega t}$:

$$e^{j\omega T_0} = 1 \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \omega T_0 = 2\pi k,\; k\in\mathbb{Z}$$

because $e^{j\alpha}=1$ exactly when $\alpha$ is a multiple of $2\pi$. Solving, $\omega = 2\pi k/T_0 = k\omega_0$.

SolutionLargest Common Frequency
Ratio test

$f_1 = a f_0$ and $f_2 = b f_0$ with integers $a, b$ forces $f_2/f_1 = b/a$ to be rational. Check:

$$\tfrac{f_2}{f_1} = \tfrac{87.5}{50} = \tfrac{87.5/12.5}{50/12.5} = \tfrac{7}{4} \in \mathbb{Q}\;\checkmark$$

Conversely, a rational ratio $b/a$ gives the common $f_0 = f_1/a$, so the answer to the first question is yes.

Largest common frequency

Any common $f_0$ has $b/a = 7/4$, so $(a,b) = (4m, 7m)$ for $m = 1,2,3,\ldots$ and $f_0 = f_1/a = 50/(4m) = 12.5/m$. The largest is $m=1$ (this is $\gcd(50, 87.5)=12.5$ Hz). Check:

$$\begin{aligned}4 f_0 &= 4(12.5) = 50 = f_1\\ 7 f_0 &= 7(12.5) = 87.5 = f_2\end{aligned}$$
Other valid choices

From $f_0 = 12.5/m$: $m=2$ gives $6.25$ Hz, $m=3$ gives $4.1\overline{6}$ Hz, $m=4$ gives $3.125$ Hz, and so on, for any positive integer $m$.

$12.5$ Hz is the largest and is the fundamental: $T_0 = 1/12.5 = 0.08$ s. The others over-count harmonics that don't actually appear.

ApplicationWhy Harmonics: a Musical Note
Application · sound

One pitch, many harmonics.

A sustained note on an instrument is periodic. The pitch we hear is $f_0$; the instrument's character (its timbre) is the set of strengths $|S_k|$ of the harmonics.

Illustration · pitch A below middle C

$f_0=220$ Hz, so $T_0=\tfrac{1}{220}=4.545$ ms and $\omega_0=2\pi(220)=1382.3$ rad/s.

Harmonic frequencies
$$\begin{aligned}k=1&:\;220\text{ Hz},\;\omega_1=1382.3\text{ rad/s}\\k=2&:\;440\text{ Hz},\;\omega_2=2764.6\text{ rad/s}\\k=3&:\;660\text{ Hz},\;\omega_3=4146.9\text{ rad/s}\\k=4&:\;880\text{ Hz},\;\omega_4=5529.2\text{ rad/s}\end{aligned}$$

Two instruments playing the same pitch share $\omega_0$ and differ only in the $S_k$. Fourier series is the tool that separates them.

ConceptThe Complex Fourier Series
Synthesis equation

Periodic $s(t)$ as a sum of harmonics.

$$s(t) \,=\, \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} S_k\, e^{j k \omega_0 t}$$
Real-valued $s$
$$S_{-k} \,=\, S_{k}^{*} \;\;\text{for all } k \in \mathbb{Z}$$

Why: $s^*=s$ gives $\sum_k S_{-k}^* e^{jk\omega_0 t} = \sum_k S_k e^{jk\omega_0 t}$ (conjugate, then relabel $k\to-k$); match coefficients.

Convergence

Infinite sum — may not converge for all $s$. We'll work with signals for which it does.

Notation: $z^*$ is the complex conjugate, $(a+jb)^*=a-jb$, so $(3e^{j0.3})^*=3e^{-j0.3}$. $\mathrm{Re}\,z=a$ is the real part. $\mathbb{Z}$ = all integers.

ConceptENES-222 · L18
ConceptDFT and Fourier Series Side by Side
Same idea, two settings

Synthesis equations compared.

DFT (L12)
$$x[n]=\tfrac1N\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}X[k]\,e^{j2\pi kn/N}$$

Finite signal, discrete time. Exactly $N$ terms. Frequencies $\Omega_k=2\pi k/N$ rad/sample. Real $x$ iff $X[N-k]=X[k]^{*}$.

Fourier series (today)
$$s(t)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}S_k\,e^{jk\omega_0 t}$$

Periodic signal, continuous time. Infinitely many terms. Frequencies $\omega_k=k\omega_0$ rad/s. Real $s$ iff $S_{-k}=S_k^{*}$.

  • Here $k$ runs over all integers, because there is no "last" harmonic; $S_{-k}$ plays the role of $X[N-k]$.
  • The DFT carries a factor $\tfrac1N$ in synthesis; in the Fourier series the factor is absorbed into $S_k$.

Worked ExampleBandlimited Series
Worked example

Three cosines and a DC offset.

$$\begin{aligned}s(t) = 5.0 &+ 6.2\cos(25\pi t + 0.3)\\ &+ 4.6\cos(100\pi t - 1.9)\\ &+ 2.8\cos(175\pi t + 2.5)\end{aligned}$$
Step 1 · Fundamental

From the practice: $f_0 = 12.5$ Hz, $T_0 = 0.08$ s, so $\omega_0 = 2\pi f_0 = 25\pi$ rad/s. Then the three frequencies are

$$25\pi = 1\cdot\omega_0,\quad 100\pi = 4\omega_0,\quad 175\pi = 7\omega_0$$
Step 2 · Cosine to two exponentials

Euler: $\cos x = \tfrac12(e^{jx}+e^{-jx})$, so for amplitude $A$, phase $\phi$:

$$A\cos(k\omega_0 t + \phi) = \tfrac{A}{2}e^{j\phi}\,e^{jk\omega_0 t} + \tfrac{A}{2}e^{-j\phi}\,e^{-jk\omega_0 t}$$
Step 3 · Coefficients, term by term

Each amplitude halves: $6.2/2 = 3.1$, $4.6/2 = 2.3$, $2.8/2 = 1.4$; the DC term is its own $S_0$.

$$\begin{aligned}5.0 &\Rightarrow S_0 = 5\\ 6.2\cos(\omega_0 t+0.3) &\Rightarrow S_{\pm1} = 3.1\,e^{\pm j0.3}\\ 4.6\cos(4\omega_0 t-1.9) &\Rightarrow S_{\pm4} = 2.3\,e^{\mp j1.9}\\ 2.8\cos(7\omega_0 t+2.5) &\Rightarrow S_{\pm7} = 1.4\,e^{\pm j2.5}\end{aligned}$$

Rectangular: $S_1 = 2.962 + 0.916j$, $S_4 = -0.744 - 2.176j$, $S_7 = -1.122 + 0.838j$; $S_{-k} = S_k^*$. All other $S_k = 0$.

SolutionFind the Fourier Coefficients
Solution · ≈ 5 min

$f_0=10$ Hz, $k=2$ and $5$.

Step 1 · frequencies, f0, T0
$40\pi=2\pi(20)$ and $100\pi=2\pi(50)$, so $20$ Hz and $50$ Hz. $\gcd(20,50)=10$ Hz: $$T_0=\tfrac{1}{10}=0.1\text{ s},\quad\omega_0=2\pi(10)=20\pi\text{ rad/s}$$
Step 2 · harmonic numbers
$40\pi=2\omega_0$ and $100\pi=5\omega_0$: $k=2$ and $k=5$.
Step 3 · sine to cosine
$\sin x=\cos(x-\tfrac{\pi}{2})$, so $\sin(100\pi t)=\cos(5\omega_0t-\tfrac{\pi}{2})$: $A=1$, $\phi=-\tfrac{\pi}{2}$.
Step 4 · coefficients, half the amplitude
$$\begin{aligned}S_0&=2\\S_{\pm2}&=\tfrac32e^{\pm j\pi/3}=0.75\pm1.299j\\S_{\pm5}&=\tfrac12e^{\mp j\pi/2}=\mp0.5j\end{aligned}$$ So $S_5=-0.5j$, $S_{-5}=+0.5j$; all others are $0$.
Step 5 · check at t = 0
$s(0)=2+3\cos\tfrac{\pi}{3}+\sin0=2+1.5+0=3.5$. Series: $S_0+2\mathrm{Re}\,S_2+2\mathrm{Re}\,S_5=2+2(0.75)+0=3.5$ ✓.

ConceptAn Infinite Series
Sawtooth wave

Infinitely many harmonics.

$$s(t) \,=\, \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\, \tfrac{2(-1)^{k+1}}{\pi k}\,\sin(k\omega_0 t)$$

Truncating to $K$ terms approximates the sawtooth $s(t) = 2t/T_0$ on $(-T_0/2, T_0/2)$, repeated every $T_0$. First coefficients of $\sin(k\omega_0 t)$:

$$\tfrac{2}{\pi} = 0.637,\;\; -\tfrac{1}{\pi} = -0.318$$ $$\tfrac{2}{3\pi} = 0.212,\;\; -\tfrac{1}{2\pi} = -0.159$$
Check at a quarter period

Then $\omega_0 t = \pi/2$ and $\sin(k\pi/2) = 1, 0, -1, 0, 1, \ldots$ for $k = 1,2,3,\ldots$ Only odd $k$ survive, and with $(-1)^{k+1}=+1$ there:

$$s = \tfrac{2}{\pi}\left(1 - \tfrac13 + \tfrac15 - \cdots\right) = \tfrac{2}{\pi}\cdot\tfrac{\pi}{4} = \tfrac12$$

The Leibniz series equals $\pi/4$, and the sawtooth gives $2t/T_0 = 2(T_0/4)/T_0 = \tfrac12$ ✓.

Gibbs phenomenon

At the jump ($t=\pm T_0/2$) every sine is $0$, so the sum is $0$, the midpoint. Just beside it, the partial sums overshoot by about $9\%$ of the jump size $2$: peak $\approx 1.18$ instead of $1$. More terms squeeze the ripple narrower, but the overshoot stays.

Worked ExampleSawtooth Coefficients
Worked example · sine series to complex form

Read off $S_k$ for the sawtooth.

Step 1 · the k-th sine term
$$\tfrac{2(-1)^{k+1}}{\pi k}\sin(k\omega_0t)$$
Step 2 · Euler
$\sin x=\tfrac{1}{2j}(e^{jx}-e^{-jx})$, so the term equals $$\tfrac{(-1)^{k+1}}{j\pi k}\,e^{jk\omega_0t}-\tfrac{(-1)^{k+1}}{j\pi k}\,e^{-jk\omega_0t}$$
Step 3 · read the coefficients
Using $\tfrac1j=-j$, for $k\ge1$: $$S_k=\tfrac{-j(-1)^{k+1}}{\pi k}=\tfrac{j(-1)^{k}}{\pi k},\qquad S_{-k}=-S_k$$ and $S_0=0$.
Step 4 · numbers and check
$$\begin{aligned}S_1&=\tfrac{-j}{\pi}=-0.318j\\S_2&=\tfrac{j}{2\pi}=0.159j\\S_3&=\tfrac{-j}{3\pi}=-0.106j\end{aligned}$$ $S_k$ is purely imaginary, so $S_{-k}=-S_k=S_k^{*}$ ✓ (real signal). And $2|S_1|=0.637=\tfrac2\pi$, the first sine amplitude ✓.

Solved ExampleSolved Example A · Square Wave
Solved Example A · square wave

Find FS of the square wave
(height $\pm1$, period $T_0$).

$x(t)=1$ for $|t|\lt T_0/4$ and $x(t)=-1$ for $T_0/4\lt|t|\le T_0/2$, repeated every $T_0$. Write $X_k$ for the coefficients (same role as $S_k$).

Step 1 · Analysis integral

The CT analog of the DFT analysis formula (inner product with $e^{jk\omega_0 t}$, averaged over one period):

$$X_k=\frac{1}{T_0}\int_{-T_0/2}^{T_0/2}x(t)\,e^{-jk\omega_0 t}\,dt,\quad \omega_0=\frac{2\pi}{T_0}$$
Step 2 · DC term
$$X_0 = \tfrac{1}{T_0}\left[(+1)\tfrac{T_0}{2} + (-1)\tfrac{T_0}{2}\right] = 0$$

(zero mean: half a period at $+1$, half at $-1$).

Step 3 · Reduce to one interval

Since $x = 2\cdot\mathbb{1}_{|t|\lt T_0/4} - 1$ (the indicator $\mathbb{1}$ is $1$ inside the stated interval and $0$ outside) and $\int_{-T_0/2}^{T_0/2} e^{-jk\omega_0 t}dt = 0$ for $k\ne0$ (a whole number of cycles):

$$X_k = \frac{2}{T_0}\int_{-T_0/4}^{T_0/4} e^{-jk\omega_0 t}\,dt$$

Solved ExampleSolved Example A · Continued
Solved Example A · continued

Evaluate and list $X_k$.

Step 4 · Integrate
$$\begin{aligned}\int_{-T_0/4}^{T_0/4} e^{-jk\omega_0 t}dt &= \frac{e^{jk\omega_0 T_0/4} - e^{-jk\omega_0 T_0/4}}{jk\omega_0}\\ &= \frac{2\sin(k\omega_0 T_0/4)}{k\omega_0}\end{aligned}$$

using $e^{jx}-e^{-jx}=2j\sin x$, and $\omega_0 T_0/4 = \pi/2$.

Step 5 · Apply the prefactor
$$X_k = \frac{2}{T_0}\cdot\frac{2\sin(k\pi/2)}{k\,(2\pi/T_0)} = \frac{2}{k\pi}\sin\!\left(\frac{k\pi}{2}\right)$$
Step 6 · Key values

$\sin(k\pi/2) = 1, 0, -1, 0, 1,\ldots$ for $k=1,2,3,4,5$:

$$X_1 = \tfrac{2}{\pi},\;\; X_2 = 0,\;\; X_3 = -\tfrac{2}{3\pi},\;\; X_4 = 0,\;\; X_5 = \tfrac{2}{5\pi}$$

Negative $k$: $X_{-k} = \frac{2\sin(-k\pi/2)}{-k\pi} = X_k$ (real and even).

Pattern

Only odd harmonics: $X_k=0$ for even $k\neq0$ (since $\sin(k\pi/2)=0$), and $X_0 = 0$.

Solved Example B · partial sum

Rebuild from two harmonics.

Use $X_{\pm1}$ and $X_{\pm3}$ from Example A ($X_0 = 0$, $X_{\pm2}=0$).

Step 1 · Truncated synthesis
$$\begin{aligned}\hat x(t)&=\sum_{|k|\leq3}X_k e^{jk\omega_0 t}\\ &= X_{-3}e^{-j3\omega_0 t}+X_{-1}e^{-j\omega_0 t}\\ &\quad+X_1 e^{j\omega_0 t}+X_3 e^{j3\omega_0 t}\end{aligned}$$
Step 2 · Pair each term with its mirror

$X_{-k} = X_k^*$ (real signal; here $X_k$ is real). With $z + z^* = 2\,\mathrm{Re}\,z$ and $e^{j\theta}+e^{-j\theta}=2\cos\theta$:

$$\hat x(t)=2\mathrm{Re}\{X_1 e^{j\omega_0 t}\}+2\mathrm{Re}\{X_3 e^{j3\omega_0 t}\}$$ $$=2\cdot\tfrac{2}{\pi}\cos(\omega_0 t)+2\cdot\left(-\tfrac{2}{3\pi}\right)\cos(3\omega_0 t)$$ $$=\frac{4}{\pi}\cos(\omega_0 t)-\frac{4}{3\pi}\cos(3\omega_0 t)$$
Check and Gibbs

At $t=0$: $\hat x = \tfrac{4}{\pi}-\tfrac{4}{3\pi} = \tfrac{8}{3\pi} = 0.849$ vs. $x(0)=1$. Adding $k=\pm5$ ($+\tfrac{4}{5\pi}\cos 5\omega_0 t$) gives $1.103$: closer. Overshoot persists near the jumps (Gibbs).

Common MistakesFour Ways to Lose Marks
Common mistakes

Check these before you hand in.

1 · Forgetting the half

$A\cos(k\omega_0t+\phi)$ gives $S_{\pm k}$ of size $A/2$, not $A$. $6.2\cos(\cdot)$ has $|S_{\pm1}|=3.1$.

2 · Dropping negative k

Every cosine needs both $S_k$ and $S_{-k}=S_k^{*}$. Check at $t=0$: $s(0)=S_0+2\sum_{k\ge1}\mathrm{Re}\,S_k$.

3 · Hz versus rad/s

$f_0=12.5$ Hz means $\omega_0=2\pi f_0=25\pi\approx78.5$ rad/s, not $12.5$. Take the gcd in Hz, then convert.

4 · Sine treated as cosine

$\sin(k\omega_0t)$ has phase $-\pi/2$: $S_{\pm k}=\mp\tfrac{A}{2}j$, purely imaginary. Using $A/2$ real is wrong.

And remember: a sum of sinusoids is periodic only if the frequency ratios are rational.
