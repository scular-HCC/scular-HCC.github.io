ConceptWhen Does $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ Have a Unique Solution?
The shape of $A$ decides

Three cases for $A\,(m \times n)$.

$m < n$ · Under-determined
A solution almost always exists.
But it is not unique — input cannot be recovered.
$m > n$ · Over-determined
A random $\mathbf{b}$ almost certainly has no solution.
Most outputs are unreachable.
$m = n$ · Square
Almost certainly: every $\mathbf{b}$ has a unique $\mathbf{x}$.
$A$ is nonsingular and $\mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{b}$.
Exception
Singular square matrices.
Some $\mathbf{b}$ have no solution; others have many.

ConceptNonsingular & the Inverse
Definition

$A$ is nonsingular iff $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$
has a unique solution for every $\mathbf{b}$.

$$A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\;\Leftrightarrow\;\mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{b}$$
$$A\,A^{-1} \,=\, A^{-1}\,A \,=\, I$$

The mapping $\mathbf{b} \mapsto \mathbf{x}$ is itself linear, so it has its own matrix — that's $A^{-1}$.

Useful identities
$$(A^{-1})^{-1} = A$$ $$(A^{\top})^{-1} = (A^{-1})^{\top}$$ $$(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$$
Permutation matrices
$$P^{-1} \,=\, P^{\top}$$

From last lecture: $P^{\top}P = I$.

ConceptDiagonal & Triangular Inverses
Easy cases

Diagonals invert entry-by-entry.
Triangles invert by substitution.

Diagonal
$$D = \mathrm{diag}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma),\;\; \alpha\beta\gamma \neq 0$$ $$D^{-1} = \mathrm{diag}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{\alpha},\tfrac{1}{\beta},\tfrac{1}{\gamma}\right)$$
Triangular

Lower triangular $L$ is nonsingular iff every diagonal entry is nonzero.

Solve $L\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ by forward substitution: $x_1 \to x_2 \to \cdots \to x_n$.

Upper triangular $U$: backward substitution: $x_n \to x_{n-1} \to \cdots \to x_1$.

Worked ExampleForward Substitution
Worked example

Invert $L$ via forward substitution.

$$L = \begin{bmatrix}2 & 0 & 0 \\ 5 & -1 & 0 \\ 1 & -2 & 3\end{bmatrix}$$
Row 1
$$2 x_1 = b_1 \;\Rightarrow\; x_1 = \tfrac{1}{2} b_1$$
Row 2
$$5 x_1 - x_2 = b_2 \;\Rightarrow\; x_2 = \tfrac{5}{2} b_1 - b_2$$
Row 3
$$x_1 - 2 x_2 + 3 x_3 = b_3 \;\Rightarrow\; x_3 = \tfrac{3}{2} b_1 - \tfrac{2}{3} b_2 + \tfrac{1}{3} b_3$$
Stack into a matrix
$$L^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix}\tfrac{1}{2} & 0 & 0 \\ \tfrac{5}{2} & -1 & 0 \\ \tfrac{3}{2} & -\tfrac{2}{3} & \tfrac{1}{3}\end{bmatrix}$$

ConceptGaussian Elimination
The principle

Reduce to triangular,
then back-substitute.

$$A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\;\Leftrightarrow\;(MA)\mathbf{x} = M\mathbf{b}$$

for any nonsingular $M$

Multiply by row-operation matrices until the system becomes upper triangular $U\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{c}$. Then solve by backward substitution.

Phase 1 · Forward elimination

In step $j$, use the $j$-th equation to zero out the $j$-th column below the diagonal. The denominators are the diagonal entries — the pivots.

Phase 2 · Backward substitution

Solve the resulting upper-triangular system from the bottom row up.

Worked ExampleSolve $3\times 3$ by Elimination
Worked example

Forward elimination,
then back-substitute.

$$\begin{bmatrix}2 & 1 & -1 \\ 4 & 0 & -1 \\ -8 & 2 & 3\end{bmatrix}\!\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix}6 \\ 6 \\ -10\end{bmatrix}$$
Step 1 · Kill $x_1$

Pivot $= 2$. Multipliers $m_{21} = -2$, $m_{31} = 4$.

$$\begin{bmatrix}2 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & -2 & 1 \\ 0 & 6 & -1\end{bmatrix}\!\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix}6 \\ -6 \\ 14\end{bmatrix}$$
Step 2 · Kill $x_2$

Pivot $= -2$. Multiplier $m_{32} = 3$.

$$\begin{bmatrix}2 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & -2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2\end{bmatrix}\!\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix}6 \\ -6 \\ -4\end{bmatrix}$$
Back-sub → $\mathbf{x}$
$$2 x_3 = -4 \Rightarrow x_3 = -2$$ $$-2 x_2 + x_3 = -6 \Rightarrow x_2 = 2$$ $$2 x_1 + x_2 - x_3 = 6 \Rightarrow x_1 = 1$$

$\mathbf{x} = (1,\,2,\,-2)^{\top}$

ConceptPivots Decide Singularity
Reading the pivots

One nonzero pivot per step.
That is the test.

Nonsingular

Every forward-elimination step finds a nonzero pivot (after a row swap if needed), and the last equation's $x_n$ coefficient is nonzero.

Singular

At some step every remaining equation has a zero coefficient for the current variable — or the final $x_n$ coefficient is zero.

In code: partial pivoting (swap in the largest-magnitude available pivot) keeps the procedure numerically stable even when the matrix is far from singular.

SolutionThree Singular $A$'s
Solution

Each $A$ has a dependent column.

$A_1 = 0$

Every $A_1 \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$. Any $\mathbf{b} \neq \mathbf{0}$ has no solution. And $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ is one solution to $A_1\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ — but so is every other $\mathbf{x}$.

$A_2$

Image is the horizontal axis. $\mathbf{b} = (0, 1)$ has no solution. The null space includes $\mathbf{x} = (0, c)$ for every $c$.

$A_3$

Row 2 is twice row 1, so the image is the line through $(1, 2)$. $\mathbf{b} = (1, 0)$ has no solution. The null space includes $\mathbf{x} = (3, -1)\,c$.

Pattern

When the columns of $A$ are linearly dependent, the image is a proper subset of $\mathbb{R}^{n}$: some $\mathbf{b}$ are unreachable, and the null space is non-trivial.

In elimination

Each of $A_1, A_2, A_3$ produces a zero pivot in forward elimination with no row swap able to fix it. That's the algorithmic signature of singularity.

Lecture 09Summary
Take with you

Five facts about solving $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$.

01 · NONSINGULAR
Unique $\mathbf{x}$ for every $\mathbf{b}$.
$A^{-1}$ exists; $\mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{b}$.
02 · INVERSE RULES
$(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$
$(A^{\top})^{-1} = (A^{-1})^{\top}$.
03 · TRIANGULAR
Nonsingular iff all diagonal entries $\neq 0$.
Solve by forward / backward substitution.
04 · GAUSSIAN ELIMINATION
Forward elimination → $U\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{c}$.
Back-substitute to recover $\mathbf{x}$.
05 · PIVOTS
Exactly one nonzero pivot per step.
Otherwise $A$ is singular.
06 · WHEN STUCK
Row swap.
Records as a permutation $P$.
