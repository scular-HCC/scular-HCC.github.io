Angles & Projection

ConceptInner Product · Norm
Inner product and norm

Length is the inner product
of a vector with itself.

$$\langle a,b\rangle \,=\, a^{\top}b \,=\, \sum_{i=1}^{m} a_i b_i$$
$$\|a\| \,=\, \sqrt{\langle a,a\rangle} \,=\, \sqrt{\textstyle\sum_i a_i^{2}}$$

Symmetric: $\langle a,b\rangle = \langle b,a\rangle$.

Linear in either slot: $\langle c_1 a^{(1)} + c_2 a^{(2)},\, b\rangle = c_1\langle a^{(1)},b\rangle + c_2\langle a^{(2)},b\rangle$.

Scaling: $\|ca\| = |c|\,\|a\|$.

ConceptWhy the Angle Formula Always Works
Why the angle formula always works

Cauchy–Schwarz, from the projection picture.

Split b into two perpendicular parts

With $\lambda=\langle a,b\rangle/\|a\|^{2}$, write $b=\lambda a+(b-\lambda a)$. The parts are perpendicular, so by Pythagoras

$$\|b\|^{2}=\lambda^{2}\|a\|^{2}+\|b-\lambda a\|^{2}$$
Drop the second term (it is at least 0)
$$\|b\|^{2}\ge\lambda^{2}\|a\|^{2}=\frac{\langle a,b\rangle^{2}}{\|a\|^{2}}$$$$\Rightarrow\;|\langle a,b\rangle|\le\|a\|\,\|b\|$$
Consequence

Dividing by $\|a\|\|b\|$ gives $|\cos\theta|\le1$, so the angle $\theta=\arccos(\cdot)$ always exists.

Numbers for the same a and b
$$|\langle a,b\rangle|=3\le\sqrt{14}\sqrt{21}=\sqrt{294}\approx17.15$$$$\cos\theta=\tfrac{3}{17.15}\approx0.175$$$$\theta\approx1.395\text{ rad}\approx79.9^{\circ}$$

ConceptOrthogonality & Angle
Orthogonality and angle

$\langle a,b\rangle = 0$ means perpendicular.
Otherwise it tells you the angle.

Orthogonality
$$a \perp b \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \langle a,b\rangle = 0$$
Pythagoras, step by step
$$\begin{aligned}\|a+b\|^{2} &= \langle a+b,\,a+b\rangle\\ &= \|a\|^{2} + 2\langle a,b\rangle + \|b\|^{2}\end{aligned}$$

Expand using linearity in each slot, and $\langle b,a\rangle=\langle a,b\rangle$ (symmetry).

$$a\perp b \;\Rightarrow\; \langle a,b\rangle = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \|a+b\|^{2} = \|a\|^{2} + \|b\|^{2}$$
Angle
$$\cos\theta \,=\, \frac{\langle a,b\rangle}{\|a\|\,\|b\|},\;\; \theta \in [0,\pi]$$

Here $\theta$ is the angle between the two arrows when they are drawn from the same point. The next slide derives this formula.

Since $\cos\theta=0$ exactly when $\theta=\pi/2$, this agrees with $\langle a,b\rangle=0$ meaning perpendicular.

ConceptProjection of $b$ onto $a$
Projection

Find $\lambda a$ so that $b - \lambda a$
is perpendicular to $a$.

$$\langle b-\lambda a,\,a\rangle = 0$$ $$\langle b,a\rangle - \lambda\langle a,a\rangle = 0$$ $$\lambda \,=\, \frac{\langle a,b\rangle}{\|a\|^{2}}$$

$\lambda$ is a scalar. The shadow of $b$ along $a$ has length $\|b\|\cos\theta$ (right triangle) and also $\lambda\|a\| = \langle a,b\rangle/\|a\|$. Equating them and dividing by $\|b\|$ gives $\cos\theta = \langle a,b\rangle/(\|a\|\|b\|)$.

ConceptRange & Projection onto $\mathcal{R}(V)$
Range of a matrix

The image of $V$ is the set
of all linear combinations of its columns.

$$\mathcal{R}(V) \,=\, \{V c \,:\, c \in \mathbb{R}^{n}\}$$

Here $V=[v^{(1)}\cdots v^{(n)}]$ is $m\times n$, so $Vc = c_1v^{(1)}+\dots+c_nv^{(n)}$. $\mathcal{R}(V)$ is a linear subspace of $\mathbb{R}^{m}$: a flat through the origin (line, plane, hyperplane).

If the columns of $V$ are pairwise orthogonal, projection onto $\mathcal{R}(V)$ reduces to summing the one-column projections.

Closest point in the range to s
$$\hat{s} \,=\, \sum_{j=1}^{n} c_j\, v^{(j)},\;\; c_j \,=\, \frac{\langle v^{(j)},\, s\rangle}{\|v^{(j)}\|^{2}}$$
ConceptENES-222 · L10
ConceptWhy $c_j$ Is Right
Check that the error is perpendicular

The residual is orthogonal to every column.

Take any column i
$$\langle v^{(i)},\, s-\hat{s}\rangle = \langle v^{(i)},s\rangle - \sum_{j} c_j\langle v^{(i)},v^{(j)}\rangle$$

Linearity in the second slot.

Orthogonal columns kill j not equal i
$$\langle v^{(i)},v^{(j)}\rangle = 0 \;(j\ne i)\;\Rightarrow\; \langle v^{(i)},\, s-\hat{s}\rangle = \langle v^{(i)},s\rangle - c_i\|v^{(i)}\|^{2}$$ $$= \langle v^{(i)},s\rangle - \langle v^{(i)},s\rangle = 0$$

So $s-\hat{s}\perp\mathcal{R}(V)$: the same condition that defined $\lambda$ for one vector, now for every column. That is why $\hat{s}$ is the closest point.

ConceptOrthogonal Columns Solve Vc = s Directly
Why orthogonal columns need no elimination

Solve $Vc=s$ by inner products.

Each coefficient decouples
$$Vc=s\;\Rightarrow\;\langle v^{(i)},Vc\rangle=\langle v^{(i)},s\rangle$$$$c_i\|v^{(i)}\|^{2}=\langle v^{(i)},s\rangle$$$$c_i=\frac{\langle v^{(i)},s\rangle}{\|v^{(i)}\|^{2}}$$
So V is nonsingular

If $Vc=0$, the same line with $s=0$ gives $c_i\|v^{(i)}\|^{2}=0$, hence $c_i=0$ for every $i$ (nonzero columns).

Tiny example: columns (1,1) and (1,-1)
$$\langle v^{(1)},v^{(2)}\rangle=1-1=0,\;\;\|v^{(j)}\|^{2}=2$$$$s=(5,1):\;\;c_1=\tfrac{5+1}{2}=3,\;\;c_2=\tfrac{5-1}{2}=2$$$$3(1,1)+2(1,-1)=(5,1)\;\checkmark$$

This $2\times2$ matrix is the smallest Fourier matrix ($N=2$).

Common MistakesFour Traps
Common mistakes · ≈ 2 min

Four traps, each with a number that exposes it.

1 · Dividing by the norm, not its square

For $a=(3,1)$, $b=(1,2.5)$: right $\lambda=5.5/10=0.55$. Wrong: $5.5/\sqrt{10}\approx1.739$ gives $\|\hat b\|=5.5$, longer than $\|b\|\approx2.69$, impossible.

2 · Checking perpendicular to the projection only

The residual must be perpendicular to every column of $V$, not just to $\hat s$ (check each $\langle v^{(i)},s-\hat s\rangle=0$).

3 · Summing projections of non-orthogonal columns

$v^{(1)}=(1,0)$, $v^{(2)}=(1,1)$, $s=(0,2)$. Sum of one-column projections: $(0,0)+(1,1)=(1,1)\ne s$. The true coefficients are $c=(-2,2)$: $-2(1,0)+2(1,1)=(0,2)$.

4 · Dropping the sign of the coefficient

A negative $\lambda$ (slide 12: $\lambda=-1$) means $\cos\theta<0$, an obtuse angle. Keep the sign; the projection points opposite to $a$.

BridgeWhat Breaks with Complex Entries?
Bridge · ≈ 2 min

Try the same formula on $v=(1,\,j)^{\top}$, with $j^{2}=-1$.

The transpose fails
$$v^{\top}v=1^{2}+j^{2}=1-1=0$$

A nonzero vector of "length" $0$: the norm and projection formulas break (they divide by $\|v\|^{2}$).

The fix: conjugate first
$$v^{H}v=|1|^{2}+|j|^{2}=1+1=2>0$$

Next lecture: $\langle v,w\rangle=v^{H}w$, and everything from today carries over.
