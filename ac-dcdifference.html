AC-DC Difference

AC-DC Difference is a measurement used to determine the thermal equivalence between DC and AC values. This is most accurately done using a thermal transfer standard (TTS) which can take on many forms.

The root-mean-square (RMS) value of an ac signal is defined by its heating effect: an ac voltage has an RMS value of 1 V when it dissipates the same average power in a resistor as 1 V dc. DC voltage and current can be measured very accurately, all the way back to quantum standards (the Josephson voltage standard and the quantum Hall resistance standard). AC quantities are much harder to measure directly. A thermal converter bridges this gap: it lets us compare an ac signal to a known dc signal by their heating effect, so the accuracy of dc measurements can be "transferred" to ac.

No real transfer device is perfect, so the ac and dc inputs that give the same output are never exactly equal. The ac-dc difference, \(\delta\), quantifies this imperfection:

\[ \delta = \frac{Q_{ac} - Q_{dc}}{Q_{dc}}, \qquad Q_{dc} = \frac{Q_{dc+} + Q_{dc-}}{2} \]

where \(Q_{ac}\) is the RMS ac input (voltage or current) and \(Q_{dc}\) is the dc input, averaged over both polarities, that produces the same output from the device. The value is very small and is normally expressed in µV/V (parts per million) for voltage or µA/A for current. If \(\delta\) is positive, the device needs slightly more ac than dc to give the same output.

Once the ac-dc difference of a converter is known, the converter can be used to measure an unknown ac quantity in terms of dc: \(Q_{ac} = Q_{dc}(1 + \delta)\).

Single Junction Thermal Converters (SJTC)

An SJTC is a thermoelement: a short heater wire with a single thermocouple bonded to its midpoint, sealed in an evacuated glass bulb to reduce heat loss and air currents. Current through the heater raises its temperature and the thermocouple produces a small dc output, typically 7 mV to 12 mV at rated input. The heater is usually rated for a few milliamps (commonly 5 mA or 10 mA), so higher voltages require a series range resistor. The thermoelement and range resistor together make a thermal voltage converter (TVC). For current, the thermoelement is placed in parallel with a shunt, forming a thermal current converter (TCC).

SJTCs are inexpensive and reliable, with ac-dc differences of a few µV/V to tens of µV/V in the audio range. They are fragile: a modest overload can burn out the heater, so always know the rated input before applying a signal.

Multijunction Thermal Converters (MJTC)

An MJTC uses many thermocouples (tens to hundreds) in series along the heater. The larger number of junctions gives an output of roughly 100 mV and spreads the heater temperature measurement over its length, which greatly reduces thermoelectric effects. Modern MJTCs are built with thin-film techniques on a membrane. MJTCs have the lowest ac-dc differences available, below 1 µV/V at audio frequencies, and are the primary standards at national labs. Their response is also very close to ideal square law, with a response exponent typically \(n = 1.97\) to \(2.03\).

Solid State Converters

Solid state transfer standards replace the vacuum thermoelement with an integrated RMS sensor (heater resistors and temperature-sensing transistors on a chip) and electronic feedback. They have a much larger and more convenient output (e.g. 2 V full scale on the 792A), much better overload tolerance, and faster settling. They are the workhorse of most commercial calibration labs. Their ac-dc difference is larger and less stable than a good MJTC, so they need regular calibration.

Photonic Based

Photonic thermometry replaces the thermocouple with an optical temperature sensor (for example a silicon photonic ring resonator whose resonant wavelength shifts with temperature). Optical readout isolates the measurement electrically from the heater circuit, which is attractive for reducing coupling and for very high frequencies. This is an active research area.

Sources of AC-DC Difference

The ac-dc difference of a thermal converter is not constant with frequency. It is generally smallest in the mid-audio range (roughly 100 Hz to 20 kHz) and grows at both low and high frequencies.

RegionEffectDescription
DC Thermoelectric effects (Thomson and Peltier) DC current flowing through the heater and junction moves heat in one direction depending on polarity, so the output differs for DC+ and DC−. This is the dc reversal error. Averaging both polarities cancels it to first order, which is why \(Q_{dc}\) is always the average of DC+ and DC−.
Low frequency (below ~40 Hz) Thermal ripple The instantaneous heating power varies at twice the signal frequency. When the heater thermal time constant is not long compared to the period, the heater temperature follows the power, and the nonlinear thermocouple response no longer gives the true average. The output also becomes noisy.
Mid frequency Small residual effects Ac-dc difference is smallest and flattest here. Leakage and dielectric losses still contribute at the µV/V level.
High frequency (above ~100 kHz) Reactance, skin effect, dielectric loss, transmission line effects Stray capacitance and inductance of the range resistor and leads, skin effect in the heater, losses in insulating materials, and the electrical length of the connector and tee cause the voltage at the heater to differ from the voltage at the reference plane. Above about 1 MHz these effects dominate.

General Concepts

There are two main uses for ac-dc difference devices: measurement of other types of devices and measurement of another ac-dc device. Measurements of other types of devices can be anything from a source to a meter. I will not delve into this topic at this time.

Measurements of another ac-dc device is the most common calibration configuration. For this measurement, two ac-dc devices are connected using a tee and measure the same electrical signal (constant voltage or current). The signal is applied through a timed sequence switching through both dc polarities and ac values (DC+, AC, DC-,...). During the measurement sequence the average of the dc polarities is matched with the ac signal based on one of the devices. The difference in the output of the second device with the correction of the first considered is the ac-dc difference.

Converter response

The output \(E\) of a thermal converter is approximately proportional to a power of its input:

\[ E = k\,V^{\,n} \]

For an ideal heater and thermocouple \(n = 2\) (output proportional to power). MJTCs come very close to this, typically \(n = 1.97\) to \(2.03\), while real SJTCs typically have \(n\) between about 1.6 and 2. Solid state devices with feedback have \(n \approx 1\). Because of this, a small relative change in input gives a relative change in output \(n\) times larger:

\[ \frac{\Delta E}{E} = n\,\frac{\Delta V}{V} \]

\(n\) is found by applying dc at the test voltage and at slightly different levels and comparing the change in output to the change in input. See Measuring the Response Exponent n for the procedure and calculation.

Comparison equation

For each converter, define the normalized output difference between the ac and the averaged dc readings:

\[ \varepsilon = \frac{E_{ac} - E_{dc}}{n\,E_{dc}}, \qquad E_{dc} = \frac{E_{dc+} + E_{dc-}}{2} \]

Both converters see exactly the same ac and dc signals at the tee, so any difference between the ac and dc source levels affects both equally and cancels. The ac-dc difference of the test converter is then:

\[ \delta_{T} = \delta_{S} + \varepsilon_{S} - \varepsilon_{T} \]

If the ac source is adjusted so the standard's ac output exactly matches its average dc output (as described above), then \(\varepsilon_{S} = 0\) and \(\delta_{T} = \delta_{S} - \varepsilon_{T}\). In practice the ac is set close to equal and the small residual \(\varepsilon_S\) is included in the calculation.

Why the sources and meters don't need to be calibrated: the result depends only on ratios of readings from the same meter, and the source level cancels between the two converters. The meter's gain error drops out; only its linearity and resolution over a very small range matter.

Measuring the Response Exponent n

The response exponent \(n\) converts a change in a converter's output into a change in its input. Each converter's measured output difference \(\Delta E / E\) is divided by \(n\) to get \(\varepsilon\), so every converter in the comparison needs a known \(n\) at each test level.

Measurement procedure

  1. Set up the converter exactly as it will be used (same range, same tee and connections) and let it stabilize at the test voltage \(V_0\).
  2. Choose a small step, \(\Delta\), typically 0.1 % to 0.5 % of \(V_0\). The step must be large enough to give a clearly measurable change in output but small enough that the converter stays on the same part of its response curve.
  3. Apply dc in the sequence \(V_0,\ V_0(1+\Delta),\ V_0,\ V_0(1-\Delta),\ V_0\), using both polarities at each level, and let the output settle before each reading. The symmetric sequence cancels linear drift, just as in the ac-dc sequence.
  4. Average the DC+ and DC− outputs at each level to get \(E_0\), \(E_{+}\), and \(E_{-}\). Average the repeated \(V_0\) readings for \(E_0\).
  5. Know the applied voltages: either measure them with a DVM or rely on the source's relative step. Only the ratio \(\Delta V / V\) matters, so the source or meter needs good linearity over the small step, not absolute accuracy.
  6. Repeat at each voltage level where the converter will be used. For SJTCs, \(n\) should be measured at each level, and periodically re-checked.

Calculation

Starting from \(E = k\,V^n\), the ratio of outputs at any two input levels gives \(n\) directly:

\[ n = \frac{\ln\left(E_{2}/E_{1}\right)}{\ln\left(V_{2}/V_{1}\right)} \]

For small steps the logarithms reduce to the familiar relative-change form. Using the symmetric steps above:

\[ n \approx \frac{\left(E_{+} - E_{-}\right)/E_0}{\left(V_{+} - V_{-}\right)/V_0} \]

Using both the up and down steps (instead of only one) cancels the second-order curvature of the response, so the result is the slope right at \(V_0\).

Variation of n with input level (SJTC)

The response exponent is not a constant. For an SJTC it depends on where on its range the converter is operated. Near half of full scale (50 % FS) a typical SJTC has \(n\) close to the ideal value of 2. As the input increases toward full scale, \(n\) falls, reaching about 1.8 at 100 % FS. The table and plot below show this typical behavior (illustrative values; each converter must be measured).

Input (% of full scale)Typical n (SJTC)
50 %2.00
60 %1.97
70 %1.93
80 %1.89
90 %1.85
100 %1.80

Why n decreases at higher input:

  • Heat loss grows faster than the heater temperature. At low levels most heat leaves the heater by conduction through its leads, which is roughly linear with temperature rise. As the heater gets hotter, radiation (proportional to \(T^4\)) becomes significant, so each added milliwatt produces a smaller temperature rise.
  • Heater resistance rises with temperature. The heater wire has a positive temperature coefficient, so at higher levels the same applied voltage produces somewhat less power than the ideal \(V^2/R\).
  • Thermocouple nonlinearity. The thermocouple's sensitivity (Seebeck coefficient) changes with temperature, which also bends the output curve.

Each of these makes the output grow more slowly than \(V^2\), and the effect becomes stronger as the converter approaches its rated input. MJTCs operate with a smaller temperature rise and spread the measurement along the heater, so their \(n\) stays within about 1.97 to 2.03.

Practical consequences:

  • Measure \(n\) at the actual level used for each comparison; do not apply a single value of \(n\) to every voltage on a range.
  • If \(n\) from 100 % FS (1.8) were used at 50 % FS (where n ≈ 2), the error in \(n\) would be about 10 %, giving an error of 10 % of \(\varepsilon\). This is small only if \(\varepsilon\) is kept small.
  • SJTCs are commonly operated between about 50 % and 100 % of full scale. Below about 50 % the output becomes small (output falls roughly with the square of the input), so resolution and noise get worse.

Worked example (SJTC)

A 1 V TVC is used at full scale, \(V_0 = 1\ \text{V}\), and the step is \(\Delta = 0.1\ \%\), so \(V_{\pm} = 1.001\ \text{V}\) and \(0.999\ \text{V}\). The averaged outputs are:

InputOutput (mean of DC+ and DC−)
\(V_- = 0.999\ \text{V}\)\(E_- = 6.9874\ \text{mV}\)
\(V_0 = 1.000\ \text{V}\)\(E_0 = 7.0000\ \text{mV}\)
\(V_+ = 1.001\ \text{V}\)\(E_+ = 7.0126\ \text{mV}\)
\[ n \approx \frac{(7.0126 - 6.9874)/7.0000}{(1.001 - 0.999)/1.000} = \frac{0.003600}{0.002000} = 1.80 \]

An MJTC measured the same way would give a value within about 1.97 to 2.03. A value well outside the expected range for the converter type is a sign of a problem: drift during the measurement, an unsettled output, the wrong step size, or a damaged converter.

How well does n need to be known?

Because \(\varepsilon = (\Delta E/E)/n\), an error in \(n\) causes a proportional error in \(\varepsilon\):

\[ u(\varepsilon) = |\varepsilon|\,\frac{u(n)}{n} \]

For example, if \(\varepsilon = 20\ \mu\text{V/V}\) and \(n\) is known to 2 %, the contribution is only \(0.4\ \mu\text{V/V}\). This is why \(n\) needs to be known only to a few percent, and why the ac level is set close to the dc level: keeping \(\varepsilon\) small keeps the effect of any error in \(n\) small.

Measurement Procedure

Switching sequence

A typical sequence is AC, DC+, AC, DC−, AC, repeated several times. At each step the switch changes the input and the system waits for the converters to settle before taking readings. Settling for vacuum thermoelements is commonly 30 s to 60 s or more; solid state devices are faster.

The symmetric sequence matters: if the converter outputs are drifting linearly, averaging the AC readings on either side of a DC reading cancels the drift. Similarly, averaging DC+ and DC− removes the dc reversal error.

Typical steps

  1. Connect the standard and test converters to the tee using the correct connectors. Keep cables short and the setup tidy.
  2. Verify the range of each converter and the maximum input of the heaters before applying any signal.
  3. Apply dc and allow the converters to warm up and stabilize at the test level.
  4. Measure the response exponent \(n\) of each converter if not already known (see Measuring the Response Exponent n).
  5. Set the ac source to the test frequency and adjust its level so that the standard's ac output is close to its dc output.
  6. Run the switching sequence, recording both converter outputs at each step.
  7. Calculate \(\varepsilon_S\), \(\varepsilon_T\), and \(\delta_T\) for each cycle; report the mean and standard deviation of the mean.
  8. Repeat for each voltage and frequency point. It is good practice to repeat a point after interchanging the converters on the tee (see below).

The tee and the reference plane

The ac-dc difference is defined at a specific point, the reference plane, which is normally the center of the tee. At high frequencies the two arms of the tee are not perfectly identical and the voltage at each converter's input differs slightly from the voltage at the center. A common check is to swap the standard and the test converter between the arms of the tee and repeat the measurement; the difference indicates the tee asymmetry. Calibration results are only valid with the same connector type and tee arrangement used when the standard was calibrated (commonly Type N, GR874, or UHF connectors).

Requirements

  • All instruments in known good condition.
  • Stable normal measurement environment.
  • Note: The sources and meters do not need to be calibrated, but they need to be in good working condition. A calibration satisfies this condition, but a simple verification and characterization is sufficient.

Environment

  • Stable temperature, typically (23 ± 1) °C, with small short-term variation during the measurement. Thermal converters are temperature sensors, so room drift appears directly in the output.
  • Controlled relative humidity (commonly below 60 %) to limit leakage in range resistors and insulators.
  • Avoid drafts from HVAC vents and heat from nearby equipment directly on the converters.

Sources

  • AC source: good short-term amplitude stability over the settling and reading time. Low distortion is helpful; thermal converters respond to true RMS, but harmonics can change the high-frequency behavior.
  • DC source: low noise, stable, and able to reverse polarity cleanly. The DC+ and DC− levels should be nearly equal in magnitude.
  • Both sources should be set to nearly the same level so the converters operate at the same point on their response curve.

Meters

The meters must resolve very small changes. For an SJTC with \(E \approx 7\) mV and \(n \approx 2\), a 1 µV/V change in input produces only \( \Delta E = n\,E\,\delta \approx 2 \times 7\ \text{mV} \times 10^{-6} = 14\ \text{nV} \). This requires a nanovoltmeter or a high-resolution DVM on a low range (or a voltage-offset/difference technique). MJTCs and solid state standards have larger outputs and are easier to read.

Grounding, shielding, and connections

  • Use a single ground point (usually at the tee) to avoid ground loops.
  • Keep output leads of the converters shielded and away from the ac signal path.
  • Thermocouple outputs are low-level dc signals: use low-thermal connections and let connections settle after handling.
  • Inspect connectors for wear and damage; connector quality directly affects high-frequency results.
Protect the standards: vacuum thermoelements burn out with very little overload. Always confirm the range resistor and the maximum heater current, start from zero, and raise the level slowly.

Combining

Following the GUM, each component is converted to a standard uncertainty \(u_i\) (in µV/V). Assuming the components are independent, they are combined by root-sum-of-squares and expanded with a coverage factor \(k\), usually \(k = 2\) for approximately 95 % confidence:

\[ u_c = \sqrt{\sum_i u_i^2}, \qquad U = k\,u_c \]

Type B components with only limit values (e.g. a specification of ±a) are typically treated as rectangular distributions with \(u = a/\sqrt{3}\).

Points to take from the comparison:

  • At 1 kHz to 20 kHz the best national labs reach a few tenths of a µV/V to about 1 µV/V. This is the floor for the whole traceability chain.
  • Uncertainty grows by roughly a factor of 2 to 4 from 20 kHz to 100 kHz, and by about 10 from 100 kHz to 1 MHz. At 1 MHz even national labs are at 10 µV/V to 25 µV/V.
  • The labs' results generally agreed with the reference value within about ±1 µV/V at 1 kHz and 20 kHz, within a few µV/V at 100 kHz, and within about ±25 µV/V at 1 MHz. This agreement is what supports each lab's published Calibration and Measurement Capabilities (CMCs).
  • Calibrations a national lab performs for customers are usually somewhat larger than its key comparison values, because customer standards are less ideal than the carefully chosen traveling standards. For example, one national lab's later results in the linked SIM.EM-K6.a regional comparison were 3.2 µV/V at 1 kHz and 20 kHz, 8.5 µV/V at 100 kHz, and 17 µV/V at 1 MHz.

Basis for the values

  • Standard: taken from typical national lab customer calibrations of SJTCs (a few µV/V at audio frequencies, about 10 µV/V at 100 kHz, tens of µV/V at 1 MHz), consistent with the SIM.EM-K6.a values above. This is the largest component at every frequency.
  • Drift: well-behaved SJTCs are stable to a µV/V or two per year at audio frequencies. High-frequency values change more because of connector wear and range resistor aging. A calibration history (control chart) is the best way to set this value.
  • Level dependence: needed only when the standard is used at a different voltage than it was calibrated at. If the calibration covers the exact level, this term can be dropped.
  • Meter linearity: each meter only measures a small change (tens of µV/V) in an otherwise constant reading, so a high-resolution DVM or nanovoltmeter contributes a few tenths of a µV/V at most. It does not depend on frequency because the meters read dc.
  • Source stability: modern calibrators are very stable at audio frequencies. Stability degrades at high frequencies, which shows up as more scatter. Some labs fold this into the repeatability term instead of listing it separately; do not count it twice.
  • Connector type / tee: negligible at audio frequencies but one of the dominant terms at 1 MHz. Estimate it from the difference seen when interchanging converters on the tee and from known differences between connector types.
  • Repeatability: the standard deviation of the mean of the measured cycles. The values in the table correspond to good, well-settled measurements.
  • Environment: effect of a ±1 °C room variation on the converters during the measurement.
  • Response exponent n: \(|\varepsilon|\,u(n)/n\) with \(\varepsilon \approx 20\) µV/V and n known to about 2 % (see Measuring the Response Exponent n).

Traceability

Traceability of ac-dc standards for most labs goes to the calibration certificate that shows the link to a national lab.

Traceability of ac-dc standards at national labs is complex. There are a number of well characterized specialized standards that the national lab uses to establish ac-dc difference. These special standards might for example only be for frequencies below 400 Hz or for frequencies over 10 kHz, low voltages, low currents, etc. The specialized standards are used to create an ensemble ac-dc value which is then assigned to working standards.

Traceability chain

  1. SI volt and ohm: dc voltage and resistance are realized with the Josephson voltage standard and quantum Hall resistance standard.
  2. Primary ac-dc standards: MJTCs and specially built converters whose ac-dc difference is determined by calculation, by modeling of individual error sources, and by intercomparison of different designs.
  3. Step-up / step-down: the primary standards work at a limited range of voltage (around a few volts). To cover higher voltages, converters of adjacent ranges are compared at a voltage both can accept, and the known value is carried up range by range (the step-up process). A similar process with micropotentiometers or resistive dividers carries values down to millivolts. Current is built up the same way with a series of shunts.
  4. Working standards: national lab working standards are calibrated against the primary group and used for customer calibrations.
  5. Customer standards: the lab's transfer standard (e.g. an SJTC set or solid state standard) is calibrated at the national lab and in turn used to calibrate the lab's calibrators and meters.

International agreement

National labs compare their ac-dc standards in international key comparisons organized by the Consultative Committee for Electricity and Magnetism (CCEM), for example CCEM-K6.a for ac-dc voltage transfer. The results support each lab's Calibration and Measurement Capabilities (CMCs), published in the BIPM key comparison database (KCDB), so a calibration traced to one national lab is accepted by others.

Quantum ac voltage

Pulse-driven ac Josephson voltage standards (sometimes called JAWS) synthesize ac waveforms with quantum-accurate amplitude. These provide an independent, quantum-based check of thermal converters at low voltage and are an active area of research at national labs.

Limitations:

  • Not a practical device. A quantum ac voltage standard is very costly to buy and run. It needs superconducting Josephson junction arrays, cryogenic cooling (liquid helium or a cryocooler), specialized high-speed pulse bias electronics, and staff experienced in running it. This limits it to a few national labs. It is a research and primary-standard tool, not something a calibration lab can use in place of a thermal transfer standard.
  • Cannot be compared directly against the best existing standards. The best ac-dc standards (MJTCs and the transfer standards used in key comparisons) are characterized at levels of a few volts and with their own input loading. Quantum ac standards produce voltages up to about 2 V and can deliver only a very small current. SJTCs and MJTCs cannot be driven directly by a quantum ac standard, because their heaters need several milliamps of current to produce a usable output. Connecting them requires added amplifiers, dividers, or transformers, and those bring their own errors and uncertainty, so the comparison is no longer a direct one.
  • Useful frequency range is limited to the audio band. The quantum accuracy of the waveform holds at the junction array, inside the cryostat. The signal then travels to the room-temperature output through long leads, which act as transmission lines. At audio frequencies their effect is negligible, but at higher frequencies it changes the voltage delivered at the output. Higher frequencies are possible, but these transmission line effects must be measured and corrected for, and those corrections add uncertainty.

For these reasons, thermal converters remain the practical basis of ac-dc traceability. Quantum ac voltage standards act as an independent cross-check rather than a replacement.

TTSThermal Transfer Standard: any device used to transfer between ac and dc by heating effect.
δAC-DC difference, in µV/V or µA/A.
nResponse exponent of a converter, \(E \propto V^n\). About 2 for MJTCs (1.97 – 2.03), about 1.6 – 2 for SJTCs, about 1 for solid state.
Reference planeThe point (usually the tee center) where the ac-dc difference is defined.
DC reversal errorDifference in output between DC+ and DC− caused by thermoelectric effects.
